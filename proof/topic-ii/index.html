Topic II · Manuscript pages 10–15

From convex order to contact on every side

Why must the image polygon TR intersect every side of an invariant polygon for an N-critical map?

Topic orientationWhat this chapter proves and why it comes here

The determinant inequalities for a strict polygon turn cyclic order into finitely many sign conditions. Proposition 3.1 then rewrites polygon invariance as the componentwise support inequality ρBΦ(θ)h≤h.

A left Perron vector forces every support inequality to be attained, so TR intersects every side of R; polarity proves that every vertex of TR lies on the boundary of R. The manuscript calls the fact that these conclusions hold for every invariant polygon with at most N vertices hereditary saturation.

Reading convention. Essential definitions appear before the first statement that needs them. Complete manuscript proofs are closed by default; opening one also reveals the added line-by-line explanation.

Where each prerequisite is established

Each prerequisite is either proved in Topic I, cited to an explicitly named standard source, or proved completely on this page. The manuscript’s appendix results are placed at the point where Theorem 3.2 first uses them; their PDF labels remain unchanged.

How the results depend on one another

  1. Lemma 2.7 → Lemma 2.8: determinant conditions make strict polygonal geometry stable under small perturbations.
  2. Proposition 3.1 + Lemma A.1 → the conclusion in Theorem 3.2 that every side of R intersects TR.
  3. Lemma A.3 → the conclusion in Theorem 3.2 that every vertex of TR lies on the boundary of R.
  4. Lemma 4.1 proves that if a side of P intersects a polygon Q contained in P, then that side contains a vertex of Q.

1 · Determinant criteria for convex position

From determinant signs to stable geometry

The first four lemmas concern finite point configurations in a real plane; they do not yet assume criticality or even introduce a linear map. They replace geometric order by finitely many inequalities: strict polygonal convex position, persistence under perturbation, strict support, and monotonic polar angle. The contraction T and the minimal vertex count enter only in the later saturation theorem. There N := νpoly(T) is the smallest number of vertices among all T-invariant polygons, and the theorem formally assumes that this number is finite and that T is N-critical.Four complete proofs

  1. 2.7
    Lemma 2.7Classical result

    Triple-sign criterion for strict convex position

    Recognizes a strict polygon from consistent signs of oriented triples.

    Definitions before the resultTerms first used here
    Oriented determinant
    For vectors a=(a₁,a₂) and b=(b₁,b₂), det(a,b)=a₁b₂−a₂b₁. It is positive exactly when the turn from a to b is counterclockwise through an angle strictly between 0 and π. Equivalently, det(q−p,z−p)>0 places z strictly to the left of the directed line p→q.
    Cyclic triple
    A triple of distinct indices in the prescribed cyclic list (z₀,…,zₘ₋₁), encountered in that order while the indices increase modulo m. Equivalently, after a cyclic relabelling, the three indices can be written 0≤i<j<k<m. This is a property of the displayed order; it does not assume that the points already form a polygon.
    Exposed edge
    A segment is an exposed edge of a convex hull when one supporting line meets the hull in exactly that segment. A supporting line “exposes” a face F if the face is exactly the set where the supporting inequality is tight. In this lemma the consistent determinant sign puts every other displayed point strictly on one side of each consecutive segment, so that segment must be a boundary edge.

    Complete statement

    Lemma 2.7 (Triple-sign criterion for strict convex position). Let z0,…,zm−1z_0,\ldots,z_{m-1} be a cyclic list of points, m≥3m\geq3. The list is exactly the cyclic vertex list of a strict convex mm-gon, in the displayed orientation, if and only if there is a sign ε∈{1,−1}\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\} such that ε⋅det⁡(zj−zi,zk−zi)>0\begin{equation} \label{eq:triple-sign-criterion} \varepsilon\cdot\det(z_j-z_i,z_k-z_i)>0 \end{equation}(2.9) for every triple of distinct indices i,j,ki,j,k occurring in that cyclic order. The displayed orientation is positive precisely when ε=1\varepsilon=1. Here and below, indices attached to a cyclic vertex, side, or normal list are read modulo the length of that list. More explicitly, three distinct classes i,j,k∈ℤ/mℤi,j,k\in\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z occur in that cyclic order when they have integer representatives i∼,j∼,k∼\widetilde i,\widetilde j,\widetilde k with i∼<j∼<k∼<i∼+m.\widetilde i<\widetilde j<\widetilde k<\widetilde i+m.

    ProofOpen the complete proof of Lemma 2.7

    Proof. If the list is the cyclic vertex list of a strict polygon, apply Lemma 2.6 in the displayed orientation; reversing orientation changes all determinant signs simultaneously.

    Conversely, first take ε=1\varepsilon=1. The inequalities force the points to be distinct and no three of them to be collinear. Fix ii. For every k∉{i,i+1}k\notin\{i,i+1\}, the indices i,i+1,ki,i+1,k occur in positive cyclic order, so det⁡(zi+1−zi,zk−zi)>0.\det(z_{i+1}-z_i,z_k-z_i)>0.     Why the common interval existsThe finite-continuity argument, without shorthand

    Output 1 — the points remain in strict convex position

    Lemma 2.7 describes strict convex position by finitely many positive triple determinants. Each determinant depends continuously on τ, so every one that is positive at 0 remains positive on some interval about 0. Their common interval preserves the displayed cyclic polygon.

    Output 2 — the selected points remain in side interiors

  3. 2.9
    Lemma 2.9Classical result

    Support-face test for strict polygons

    Tests whether a supporting line touches a strict polygon only at the chosen vertex.

    Definitions before the resultTerms first used here
    Direction vector of a line
    Any nonzero vector parallel to the line. A point y lies on the line through x with direction v exactly when det(v,y−x)=0.

    Complete statement

    Lemma 2.9 (Support-face test for strict polygons). Let PP be a strict polygon with cyclic vertices xix_i, and let LL be a supporting line of PP through xix_i. Then LL is a strict supporting line at xix_i if and only if neither adjacent vertex xi−1x_{i-1} nor xi+1x_{i+1} lies on LL. Equivalently, once support is known, strictness is characterized by the two nonzero determinants det⁡(v,xi−1−xi)≠0,det⁡(v,xi+1−xi)≠0,\det(v,x_{i-1}-x_i)\neq0, \qquad \det(v,x_{i+1}-x_i)\neq0, where vv is any nonzero direction vector of LL.

    ProofOpen the complete proof of Lemma 2.9

    Proof. The contact set P∩LP\cap L is a face of the polygon. If it is more than the single point xix_i, it contains a nondegenerate boundary segment having xix_i as one endpoint. In a strict polygon the maximal nondegenerate faces are exactly the displayed sides, so this segment lies in either [xi−1,xi][x_{i-1},x_i] or [xi,xi+1][x_i,x_{i+1}], and the corresponding adjacent vertex is on LL. Conversely, if an adjacent vertex lies on LL, then the contact face contains the side joining it to xix_i and the support is not strict. The determinant formulation is the line-membership test for the two adjacent vertices. ◻

2 · Support coordinates

The normal fan and its transfer matrix

We now work in the adapted Euclidean coordinate supplied by Proposition 2.1. The inner product, unit normals, and angles below refer to that coordinate. This is a coordinate choice already justified in Topic I, not a new assumption.

Outward unit normal ui
The unit vector perpendicular to side i and pointing away from the polygon. The side is the equality set in ⟨ui,z⟩≤hi.
Support number hi
The maximum hP(ui) of the functional ⟨ui,·⟩ on P. It is positive because 0 is strictly inside every side inequality.
Cone between adjacent normal rays
The nonnegative cone spanned by two adjacent normal rays. Every nonzero direction belongs to at least one such cone, and every direction not itself a fan ray belongs to exactly one.
BΦ(θ)
The nonnegative matrix whose ith row contains the two coefficients expressing the backward-rotated normal e−iθui using the two adjacent normal rays that contain it between them.

Let PP be a strict invariant polygon containing 00 in its interior. Write its outward unit normals in cyclic order as ui=eiϕi,ϕ1<⋯<ϕm<ϕ1+2π,u_i=e^{i\phi_i}, \qquad \phi_1<\cdots<\phi_m<\phi_1+2\pi, and denote the resulting cyclic normal fan by Φ=(ℝ≥0ui)i=1m,\Phi=\bigl(\mathbb R_{\geq0}u_i\bigr)_{i=1}^m, regarded with the cyclic indexing convention above. Let hi=hP(ui)>0h_i=h_P(u_i)>0 be the support numbers. For each ii, express e−iθuie^{-i\theta}u_i in the cone generated by the two adjacent fan rays that contain it: e−iθui=aiuj+biuj+1,ai,bi≥0.\begin{equation} \label{eq:sine-coefficients} e^{-i\theta}u_i=a_i u_j+b_i u_{j+1}, \qquad a_i,b_i\geq0. \end{equation}(3.1) The resulting full coefficient vector is unique and defines a nonnegative matrix BΦ(θ)B_\Phi(\theta). If the direction lies on a fan ray, either adjacent cone gives that same vector: the coefficient of the unit generator of that ray is 11 and the other displayed coefficient is 00.

3 · Geometry becomes a matrix inequality

The normal-fan transfer

One proposition translates polygon invariance into finitely many support inequalities and proves that the complex vector u of outward normals satisfies BΦ(θ)u=e−iθu.One complete proof

  1. 3.1
    Proposition 3.1Previously known

    Normal-fan transfer

    Translates invariance of a polygon into a compatible nonnegative action on its supporting normals.

    Definitions before the resultTerms first used here
    Support function and support number
    For a compact convex set P, the support function assigns to each vector u the largest value of the functional z↦⟨u,z⟩ on P. For an outward unit normal to a side, the corresponding support number is that side’s signed distance from 0 in the adapted Euclidean metric. It is positive because 0 lies in the interior.hP(u)=maxz∈P⟨u,z⟩,hi=hP(ui).
    Normal fan
    The cyclic rays generated by the outward side normals. Each direction lies in a cone between two adjacent rays; inside that cone its support value is determined by the two support inequalities meeting at the corresponding vertex.
    Nonnegative transfer matrix BΦ(θ)
    For each row i, rotate uᵢ backwards by θ and express it as aᵢuⱼ+bᵢuⱼ₊₁ using the two adjacent normal rays that contain the rotated direction between them. The only nonzero entries in row i are those coefficients aᵢ,bᵢ≥0. Thus BΦ(θ) records how the rotated directions interpolate between adjacent normal rays.
    Componentwise inequality
    The notation ρBh≤h means (ρBh)ᵢ≤hᵢ for every coordinate i. Each coordinate is precisely one supporting-side inequality for λP⊆P.

    Complete statement

    Proposition 3.1 (Normal-fan transfer). With h=(hi)h=(h_i), λP⊆P⇔ρBΦ(θ)h≤h.\begin{equation} \label{eq:fan-containment} \lambda P\subseteq P \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \rho B_\Phi(\theta)h\leq h. \end{equation}(3.2) Moreover, for the complex vector u=(ui)T∈ℂmu=(u_i)^T\in\mathbb C^m, BΦ(θ)u=e−iθu.\begin{equation} \label{eq:first-harmonic} B_\Phi(\theta)u=e^{-i\theta}u. \end{equation}(3.3) The support vectors defining strict polygons with this fixed normal fan form an open subset of ℝm\mathbb R^m.

    For openness, the two boundary lines with normals ui,ui+1u_i,u_{i+1} intersect at a vertex depending linearly on (hi,hi+1)(h_i,h_{i+1}). At a strict polygon each such vertex satisfies all nonincident side inequalities strictly, and all cyclic turn determinants have the convex nonzero sign. These are finitely many strict inequalities in continuous functions of hh and therefore persist under a sufficiently small perturbation. ◻

    The support-function calculationWhy adjacent normals determine one support value

    Let the side inequalities meeting at a vertex v be ⟨uj, z⟩ ≤ hj and ⟨uj+1, z⟩ ≤ hj+1. If q = auj + buj+1 with a, b ≥ 0, then every z in the polygon satisfies

    ⟨q, z⟩ ≤ a hj + b hj+1.

    At their common vertex v, both side inequalities are equalities, so the displayed upper bound is attained. It is therefore exactly hP(q). Adjacent normal rays are linearly independent, so a and b are unique. If q itself lies on a fan ray, the coefficient on the other ray is 0; choosing either neighbouring cone gives the same coefficient vector. This is the boundary case mentioned in the manuscript.

    This support-function calculation is standard convex geometry; see Schneider, Convex Bodies, Chapter 1, §§1.3 and 1.7, and Chapter 2, §§2.1 and 2.4. Proposition 3.1 proves the particular transfer formula used here.

    This manuscript uses hereditary saturation for two conclusions that hold for every invariant polygon R with at most N vertices: every side E of R satisfies E∩TR≠∅, and every vertex of TR lies on ∂R. Here N=νpoly(T) is the minimal number of vertices among T-invariant polygons.

    For support vector h and matrix B, put q:=h−ρBh. Its coordinate qᵢ=hᵢ−(ρBh)ᵢ is nonnegative by invariance and measures the gap in side i’s support inequality. If qᵢ=0, side i intersects TR; if qᵢ>0, it does not. In optimization, such a nonnegative gap is often called slack.

    The statement E∩TR≠∅ means that the side E and the image polygon TR share at least one point.

    Vectors uᵢ positively span the plane when every vector is a nonnegative linear combination of them, equivalently when their positive cone is all of ℝ². For finitely many half-planes with these outward normals, there is no nonzero direction in which one can escape to infinity.

    Three compressed implicationsBoundedness, contact, and complementarity unpacked

    Why positive spanning makes the intersection bounded

    For RS = ∩i∈S{z : ⟨ui,z⟩ ≤ hi}. A nonzero direction d along which one could remain inside this intersection indefinitely would have to satisfy ⟨ui,d⟩ ≤ 0 for every i∈S. If the ui positively span ℝ², then d itself is a nonnegative combination of them. Pairing that combination with d gives |d|² ≤ 0, so d=0. A finite half-plane intersection with no nonzero escape direction is bounded.

    Why complementarity removes every support-inequality gap

    Put q=h−ρBh. Both w and q are coordinatewise nonnegative, while the eigenvector calculation gives Σiwiqi=0. Every summand is nonnegative, hence every summand is 0. After the theorem proves wi>0 for all i, it follows that every qi=0.

    Why equality of support values is actual side contact

    The equality hTR(ui)=hR(ui) says that the continuous functional ⟨ui,·⟩ has the same maximum on the compact sets TR and R. It attains that maximum at some point of TR, and every point of R attaining it lies on side i. Hence that side intersects TR.
