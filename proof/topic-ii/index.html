Topic II · Manuscript pages 10–15

From convex order to active sides

Why must every side of a critical polygon participate in the dynamics?

Topic orientationWhat this chapter proves and why it comes here

Strict convexity turns cyclic order into a finite system of signs and supporting inequalities. Normal-fan transfer then rewrites polygon invariance on the dual side, where a Perron argument can detect slack.

The central conclusion is hereditary saturation: not only the chosen critical polygon, but every admissible subpolygon with the same vertex budget has all of its sides active. This is a genuine strengthening of the earlier saturation theorem and is the safeguard needed for later surgery.

Reading convention. Essential definitions appear before the first statement that needs them. Complete manuscript proofs are closed by default; opening one also reveals the added line-by-line explanation.

1 · Finite convex certificates

From determinant signs to stable geometry

The first four lemmas concern finite point configurations in a real plane; they do not yet assume criticality or even introduce a linear map. They replace geometric order by finitely many inequalities: strict polygonal convex position, persistence under perturbation, strict support, and monotonic polar angle. The contraction T and its vertex budget enter only in the later saturation theorem. There N := νpoly(T) is the smallest number of vertices among all T-invariant polygons, and the theorem formally assumes that this number is finite and that T is N-critical.Four complete proofs

  1. 2.7
    Lemma 2.7Classical result

    Triple-sign criterion for strict convex position

    Recognizes a strict polygon from consistent signs of oriented triples.

    Definitions before the resultTerms first used here
    Oriented determinant
    For vectors a=(a₁,a₂) and b=(b₁,b₂), det(a,b)=a₁b₂−a₂b₁. It is positive exactly when the turn from a to b is counterclockwise through an angle strictly between 0 and π. Equivalently, det(q−p,z−p)>0 places z strictly to the left of the directed line p→q.
    Cyclic triple
    A triple of boundary points (zᵢ,zⱼ,zₖ) in increasing cyclic order around the polygon, written with indices i<j<k in one chosen starting point.
    Exposed edge
    A segment is an exposed edge of a convex hull when one supporting line meets the hull in exactly that segment. A supporting line “exposes” a face F if the face is exactly the set where the supporting inequality is tight. In this lemma the consistent determinant sign puts every other displayed point strictly on one side of each consecutive segment, so that segment must be a boundary edge.
    Complete certificate
    A finite list is a complete certificate when the listed conditions are necessary and sufficient for the target property. If every condition holds, the target property follows; if the target property fails, at least one condition must fail.

    Complete statement

    Lemma 2.7 (Triple-sign criterion for strict convex position). Let z0,…,zm−1z_0,\ldots,z_{m-1} be a cyclic list of points, m≥3m\geq3. The list is exactly the cyclic vertex list of a strict convex mm-gon, in the displayed orientation, if and only if there is a sign ε∈{1,−1}\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\} such that ε⋅det⁡(zj−zi,zk−zi)>0\begin{equation} \label{eq:triple-sign-criterion} \varepsilon\cdot\det(z_j-z_i,z_k-z_i)>0 \end{equation}(2.9) for every triple of distinct indices i,j,ki,j,k occurring in that cyclic order. The displayed orientation is positive precisely when ε=1\varepsilon=1.

    ProofOpen the complete proof of Lemma 2.7

    Proof. If the list is the cyclic vertex list of a strict polygon, apply Lemma 2.6 in the displayed orientation; reversing orientation changes all determinant signs simultaneously.

    Conversely, first take ε=1\varepsilon=1. The inequalities force the points to be distinct and no three of them to be collinear. Fix ii. For every k∉{i,i+1}k\notin\{i,i+1\}, the indices i,i+1,ki,i+1,k occur in positive cyclic order, so det⁡(zi+1−zi,zk−zi)>0.\det(z_{i+1}-z_i,z_k-z_i)>0. Thus every other point lies strictly in the left half-plane of the directed line from ziz_i to zi+1z_{i+1}. Hence [zi,zi+1][z_i,z_{i+1}] is an exposed edge of conv⁡{z0,…,zm−1}\mathop{\mathrm{conv}}\{z_0,\ldots,z_{m-1}\}. This holds for every ii, so all displayed points are extreme, the displayed consecutive segments are exactly the boundary edges, and the resulting polygon is strict and positively oriented. The case ε=−1\varepsilon=-1 is identical with left replaced by right, or follows by reversing the displayed orientation. ◻

    IntuitionConvexity can be certified without first drawing the convex hull. If every triple of boundary vertices (zᵢ,zⱼ,zₖ) of the displayed cyclic list, with i<j<k in that order, has the same nonzero orientation, then each consecutive segment sees every remaining point strictly on its inner side and must therefore be an exposed boundary edge.

      Step 1

      Necessity is imported from Lemma 2.6: positive boundary order and positive determinant describe the same orientation.

    2. 2

      Step 2

      For sufficiency, fix one consecutive pair zᵢ,zᵢ₊₁. The triple inequalities put every other point strictly in the same open half-plane.

    3. 3

      Step 3

    Keep in mindA finite list of strict determinant signs is a complete certificate in the usual sense: the listed sign conditions are all necessary, and together they are sufficient. If the points were not in strict convex position, at least one listed condition would fail.

  2. 2.8
    Lemma 2.8Classical result

    Simultaneous convex admissibility under perturbation

    Packages the finitely many strict inequalities that keep several related polygons convex during a small deformation.

    Definitions before the resultTerms first used here
    Continuous point function
    A continuous map ϕ:(−δ,δ)→V, where V is the real plane used throughout the chapter (and may be identified with ℝ² or ℂ). The word ‘point’ only says that each value ϕ(τ) is a point of that plane: as τ changes slightly, the point moves without jumps.
    Common-neighbourhood argument
    If finitely many conditions are strict and remain true at τ=0, each gives an open interval of τ where it still holds. Their finite intersection is a nonempty open interval on which all of them hold simultaneously.
    Simultaneous admissibility
    All required strict inequalities and open-segment conditions hold on one common interval around τ=0. Finiteness matters: intersecting finitely many open neighbourhoods still leaves an open neighbourhood of 0.
    Affine coordinate on a side
    A point w on the line through p and q has a unique expression w=(1−α)p+αq when p≠q. It lies in the relative interior of [p,q] exactly when 0<α<1.
    Why the common interval existsThe finite-continuity argument, without shorthand

    Suppose f1, …, fr are continuous real functions and every fj(0) is positive. For each j, continuity at 0 supplies an open interval Ij on which fj(τ) remains positive. Because there are only finitely many functions, I = I1 ∩ ··· ∩ Ir is still an open interval containing 0.

    Lemma 2.8 applies this elementary fact three times: to the triple determinants certifying strict convexity, to the inequalities 0 < αa(τ) < 1 certifying open-side membership, and to the prescribed side determinants. The sets 𝒜, ℬ, and the cyclic vertex set are finite, so one final intersection works for every requirement simultaneously.

  3. 2.9
    Lemma 2.9Classical result

    Support-face test for strict polygons

    Identifies a side through equality with a supporting functional.

    Definitions before the resultTerms first used here
    Direction vector of a line
    Any nonzero vector parallel to the line. A point y lies on the line through x with direction v exactly when det(v,y−x)=0.

    Complete statement

    Lemma 2.9 (Support-face test for strict polygons). Let PP be a strict polygon with cyclic vertices xix_i, and let LL be a supporting line of PP through xix_i. Then LL is a strict supporting line at xix_i if and only if neither adjacent vertex xi−1x_{i-1} nor xi+1x_{i+1} lies on LL. Equivalently, once support is known, strictness is certified by the two nonzero determinants det⁡(v,xi−1−xi)≠0,det⁡(v,xi+1−xi)≠0,\det(v,x_{i-1}-x_i)\neq0, \qquad \det(v,x_{i+1}-x_i)\neq0, where vv is any nonzero direction vector of LL.

    ProofOpen the complete proof of Lemma 2.9

    Proof. The contact set P∩LP\cap L is a face of the polygon. If it is more than the single point xix_i, it contains a nondegenerate boundary segment having xix_i as one endpoint. In a strict polygon the maximal nondegenerate faces are exactly the displayed sides, so this segment lies in either [xi−1,xi][x_{i-1},x_i] or [xi,xi+1][x_i,x_{i+1}], and the corresponding adjacent vertex is on LL. Conversely, if an adjacent vertex lies on LL, then the contact face contains the side joining it to xix_i and the support is not strict. The determinant formulation is the line-membership test for the two adjacent vertices. ◻

    Lemma 2.10Classical result

    Angular monotonicity

    Shows that the argument of points on a strict convex boundary increases in boundary order.

    Definitions before the resultTerms first used here
    Lifted arguments
    Angles chosen as real numbers rather than only modulo 2π. They obey the periodic lift relation below, which records an unbroken counterclockwise circuit and makes each angular gap an ordinary real number.Θi+N=Θi+2π.
    Argument along a segment
    For a nonzero differentiable path z(t), a continuous branch of its polar angle satisfies d Arg(z(t))/dt=det(z(t),z′(t))/|z(t)|². Here the side avoids 0 because 0 is an interior point and the side lies on the boundary.

2 · Support coordinates

The normal fan and its transfer matrix

We now work in the adapted Euclidean coordinate supplied by Proposition 2.1. The inner product, unit normals, and angles below refer to that coordinate. This is a coordinate choice already justified in Topic I, not a new assumption.

Outward unit normal ui
The unit vector perpendicular to side i and pointing away from the polygon. The side is the equality set in ⟨ui,z⟩≤hi.
Support number hi
The maximum hP(ui) of the functional ⟨ui,·⟩ on P. It is positive because 0 is strictly inside every side inequality.
Fan cone
The nonnegative cone spanned by two adjacent normal rays. Every nonzero direction belongs to at least one such cone, and every direction not itself a fan ray belongs to exactly one.
BΦ(θ)
The nonnegative matrix whose ith row contains the two coefficients expressing the backward-rotated normal e−iθui in its containing fan cone.

Let PP be a strict invariant polygon containing 00 in its interior. Write its outward unit normals in cyclic order as ui=eiϕi,ϕ1<⋯<ϕm<ϕ1+2π,u_i=e^{i\phi_i}, \qquad \phi_1<\cdots<\phi_m<\phi_1+2\pi, and let hi=hP(ui)>0h_i=h_P(u_i)>0 be its support numbers. For each ii, express e−iθuie^{-i\theta}u_i in the cone generated by the two adjacent fan rays that contain it: e−iθui=aiuj+biuj+1,ai,bi≥0.\begin{equation} \label{eq:sine-coefficients} e^{-i\theta}u_i=a_i u_j+b_i u_{j+1}, \qquad a_i,b_i\geq0. \end{equation}(3.1) The coefficients are unique, including the boundary case, and define a nonnegative matrix BΦ(θ)B_\Phi(\theta).

The geometric dictionaryWhy adjacent normals determine one support value

Let the side inequalities meeting at a vertex v be ⟨uj, z⟩ ≤ hj and ⟨uj+1, z⟩ ≤ hj+1. If q = auj + buj+1 with a, b ≥ 0, then every z in the polygon satisfies

⟨q, z⟩ ≤ a hj + b hj+1.

At their common vertex v, both side inequalities are equalities, so the displayed upper bound is attained. It is therefore exactly hP(q). Adjacent normal rays are linearly independent, so a and b are unique. If q itself lies on a fan ray, the coefficient on the other ray is 0; choosing either neighbouring cone gives the same coefficient vector. This is the boundary case mentioned in the manuscript.

This support-function dictionary is standard convex geometry; see Schneider, Convex Bodies, Chapter 1, §§1.3 and 1.7, and Chapter 2, §§2.1 and 2.4. Proposition 3.1 proves the particular transfer formula used here.

3 · Geometry becomes a matrix inequality

The normal-fan transfer

One proposition translates polygon invariance into finitely many support inequalities and identifies the rotating first harmonic of the transfer matrix.One complete proof

  1. 3.1
    Proposition 3.1Previously known

    Normal-fan transfer

    Translates invariance of a polygon into a compatible nonnegative action on its supporting normals.

    Definitions before the resultTerms first used here
    Support function and support number
    For a compact convex set P, the support function assigns to each vector u the largest value of the functional z↦⟨u,z⟩ on P. For an outward unit normal to a side, the corresponding support number is that side’s signed distance from 0 in the adapted Euclidean metric. It is positive because 0 lies in the interior.hP(u)=maxz∈P⟨u,z⟩,hi=hP(ui).
    Normal fan
    The cyclic rays generated by the outward side normals. Each direction lies in a cone between two adjacent rays; inside that cone its support value is determined by the two support inequalities meeting at the corresponding vertex.
    Nonnegative transfer matrix BΦ(θ)
    For each row i, rotate uᵢ backwards by θ and express it as aᵢuⱼ+bᵢuⱼ₊₁ in the containing fan cone. The only nonzero entries in row i are those coefficients aᵢ,bᵢ≥0. Thus BΦ(θ) records how the rotated directions interpolate between adjacent fan rays.
    Componentwise inequality
    The notation ρBh≤h means (ρBh)ᵢ≤hᵢ for every coordinate i. Each coordinate is precisely one supporting-side inequality for λP⊆P.

    Complete statement

    Proposition 3.1 (Normal-fan transfer). With h=(hi)h=(h_i), λP⊆P⇔ρBΦ(θ)h≤h.\begin{equation} \label{eq:fan-containment} \lambda P\subseteq P \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \rho B_\Phi(\theta)h\leq h. \end{equation}(3.2) Moreover, for u=(ui)Tu=(u_i)^T, BΦ(θ)u=e−iθu.\begin{equation} \label{eq:first-harmonic} B_\Phi(\theta)u=e^{-i\theta}u. \end{equation}(3.3) The support vectors defining strict polygons with this fixed normal fan form an open subset of ℝm\mathbb R^m.

    Proof. If v=auj+buj+1v=a u_j+b u_{j+1} belongs to the cone generated by two adjacent normal rays, with a,b≥0a,b\geq0, the two adjacent support inequalities are maximized at their common polygon vertex. For every z∈Pz\in P one therefore has ⟨v,z⟩≤ahP(uj)+bhP(uj+1),\langle v,z\rangle \leq a h_P(u_j)+b h_P(u_{j+1}), with equality at that common vertex.     For openness, the two boundary lines with normals ui,ui+1u_i,u_{i+1} meet in a vertex depending linearly on (hi,hi+1)(h_i,h_{i+1}). At a strict polygon each such vertex satisfies all nonincident side inequalities strictly, and all cyclic turn determinants have the convex nonzero sign. These are finitely many strict inequalities in continuous functions of hh and therefore persist under a sufficiently small perturbation. ◻

    The geometric dictionaryWhy adjacent normals determine one support value

    Let the side inequalities meeting at a vertex v be ⟨uj, z⟩ ≤ hj and ⟨uj+1, z⟩ ≤ hj+1. If q = auj + buj+1 with a, b ≥ 0, then every z in the polygon satisfies

    ⟨q, z⟩ ≤ a hj + b hj+1.

    At their common vertex v, both side inequalities are equalities, so the displayed upper bound is attained. It is therefore exactly hP(q). Adjacent normal rays are linearly independent, so a and b are unique. If q itself lies on a fan ray, the coefficient on the other ray is 0; choosing either neighbouring cone gives the same coefficient vector. This is the boundary case mentioned in the manuscript.

    This support-function dictionary is standard convex geometry; see Schneider, Convex Bodies, Chapter 1, §§1.3 and 1.7, and Chapter 2, §§2.1 and 2.4. Proposition 3.1 proves the particular transfer formula used here.

    Saturation
    Side saturation means every side of R has nonempty intersection with TR (equivalently, each support inequality is tight). Vertex saturation means every vertex of TR lies on ∂R. The theorem proves both, for every invariant polygon with the same vertex budget N=νpoly(T).
    Vertex budget
    The fixed value N=νpoly(T), the minimal number of vertices among T-invariant polygons (or ∞ if none exist). In this topic the budget is fixed once and for all for the contraction T under study.
    Support slack
    For support vector h and matrix B, define slack q:=h−ρBh. Each coordinate is qᵢ=hᵢ−(ρBh)ᵢ and is ≥0 by invariance. qᵢ=0 is tight saturation of side i, while qᵢ>0 means that side is not hit by TR.
    Vanishing slack
    The condition q=0, i.e. h=ρBh coordinatewise. Vanishing slack means every side inequality is tight, so every side intersects TR.
    Meets
    For sets A and B, A meets B means A∩B is nonempty. Here a polygon side meets TR if they share at least one point.
    Positive spanning
    Vectors uᵢ positively span the plane when every vector is a nonnegative linear combination of them, equivalently when their positive cone is all of ℝ². For finitely many half-planes with these outward normals, there is no nonzero direction in which one can escape to infinity.

    Three compressed implicationsBoundedness, contact, and complementarity unpacked

    Why positive spanning makes the retained polygon bounded

    For RS = ∩i∈S{z : ⟨ui,z⟩ ≤ hi}, an escape direction d would have to satisfy ⟨ui,d⟩ ≤ 0 for every i∈S. If the ui positively span ℝ², then d itself is a nonnegative combination of them.     Why complementarity removes every support slack

    Put q=h−ρBh. Both w and q are coordinatewise nonnegative, while the eigenvector calculation gives Σiwiqi=0. Every summand is nonnegative, hence every summand is 0. After the theorem proves wi>0 for all i, it follows that every qi=0.

    Why equality of support values is actual side contact

    The equality hTR(ui)=hR(ui) says that the continuous functional ⟨ui,·⟩ has the same maximum on the compact sets TR and R. It attains that maximum at some point of TR, and every point of R attaining it lies on side i. Hence that side meets TR.

    IntuitionRadial criticality leaves no unused support inequality. If the transfer inequality had spectral slack, the support vector could be perturbed to admit a slightly larger radial map. A left Perron vector then detects exactly which sides could still have slack; any missing coordinates would themselves define a smaller invariant polygon, contradicting minimal complexity.

    Lemma 4.1Previously known

    Side contact witnessed by an image vertex

    Turns saturation of a side into a concrete vertex whose image lands on that side.

    Definitions before the resultTerms first used here
    Witness
    A concrete vertex q of Q lying on the side E. The lemma upgrades the set-level statement E∩Q≠∅ to a vertex-level contact, which is the discrete datum needed later.

    Complete statement

    Write the vertices of a strict polygon PP in positive cyclic order as xix_i (i∈ℤ/Nℤi\in\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z), and put Ei=[xi−1,xi],Ei+=(xi−1,xi],Ei−=[xi−1,xi).E_i=[x_{i-1},x_i],\qquad E_i^+=(x_{i-1},x_i],\qquad E_i^-=[x_{i-1},x_i). All cyclic intervals below are taken in this fixed orientation. We shall repeatedly use the following elementary consequence of full side touching.

    Lemma 4.1 (Side contact is witnessed by an image vertex). Let Q⊆PQ\subseteq P be polygons. If a side EE of PP meets QQ, then EE contains a vertex of QQ.

    ProofOpen the complete proof of Lemma 4.1

    Proof. Let ℓ\ell be a supporting functional of PP whose maximum face is EE. If E∩Q≠⌀E\cap Q\neq\varnothing, then the maximum of ℓ\ell on QQ equals its maximum on PP. A linear functional on a polygon attains its maximum at a vertex, so a vertex of QQ belongs to EE. ◻
