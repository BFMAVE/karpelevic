Topic XI · Manuscript pages 91–93

Explicit stochastic realization of the candidate curve

How can every candidate point be realized by an actual stochastic matrix?

Topic orientationWhat this chapter proves and why it comes here

An explicit sparse row-stochastic matrix is defined by a weighted digraph. The proof derives its characteristic polynomial from pairwise vertex-disjoint directed cycles and shows that it is the reduced Ito polynomial.

For every Farey interval in Topic IX, this independently proves that the complete candidate curve lies in the corresponding Karpelevič region. The chapter then combines this inclusion with Topic X and applies the equality condition in Jensen’s inequality.

Imported from earlier topics

  • Topic IX — the candidate curve and complementary coefficients: defines the compact curve Γf,g(n) on each upper Farey interval, supplies its point at each interior argument, and gives parameters α,β with α+β=1.
  • Topic X — the upper comparison: is used only in the final deferred corollary, after stochastic attainment has been proved independently.

Standard background, stated with sources

  • Leibniz determinant expansion: determinants are sums over permutations; each permutation decomposes uniquely into vertex-disjoint cycles.
  • Block-diagonal padding: adjoining absorbing states adds eigenvalues equal to one but preserves every eigenvalue of the original stochastic block.

Proved on this page

We derive the characteristic-polynomial expansion over disjoint directed cycles, classify every directed cycle of the constructed weighted digraph, and compute its characteristic polynomial in the cases s≤dq and s>dq. This proves Γf,g(n)⊆Θₙ, including the Farey endpoints and the exceptional order-three real segment, before the equal-parameter conclusion deferred from Topic X is proved.

Notation recalled

The attainable region and its radial maximum

Here ΘN is the set of all eigenvalues of row-stochastic matrices of order N, and RN(θ) is its maximal radius on the ray of argument θ. We write ρ=RN(θ) for an attained radial maximum and ρ* for the candidate radius constructed in Topic IX.

Farey–Ito data

The polynomial to be realized

Let f<g be consecutive in Fₙ⁺=Fₙ∩[0,1/2], where Fₙ is the Farey sequence of order n. Write {f,g}={p/q,r/s} with q<s, and set d=⌊n/q⌋. Only the denominators q and s enter the matrix construction.

For α,β≥0 with α+β=1, the nonzero solutions of the Ito equation are precisely the zeros of

Pα,β(t)={(tq−β)d−αdtdq−s,s≤dq,ts−dq(tq−β)d−αd,s>dq.

Theorem II.7.3 constructs a row-stochastic matrix of order N₀=max(dq,s)≤n whose characteristic polynomial is the appropriate displayed polynomial.

Adjacency convention

Weighted-digraph representation of a row-stochastic matrix

A directed edge u→v of weight w is stored in row u and column v:

Aᵤᵥ=w(u,v). (II.7.1)

Thus the entries in row u are the weights leaving vertex u. The matrix is row-stochastic exactly when all edge weights are nonnegative and the weights leaving each vertex sum to one.

In the Leibniz expansion of det(tI−A), group each nonzero term by its nontrivial permutation cycles and any selected loops. These form pairwise vertex-disjoint directed cycles; every remaining vertex contributes a diagonal factor t. Lemma II.7.1 derives the sign and exponent directly from the permutation expansion.

XI.A

Directed-cycle expansion and graph classification

First the determinant rule is proved from Leibniz. Then the sparse realization graph is checked so that no unlisted directed cycle can contribute to its characteristic polynomial.

Lemma II.7.2 mentions the realization theorem that follows only to identify where its generic graph lemma will be applied. Its hypotheses describe the graph completely, and its proof does not assume the later theorem.

  1. II.7.1
    Lemma II.7.1Classical result

    Directed-cycle expansion of the characteristic polynomial

    Derives exactly how pairwise vertex-disjoint directed cycles contribute signs, edge weights, and powers of t to a characteristic polynomial.

    Definitions and notation used here4 entries to consult as needed
    Graph order N
    The total number of vertices of the finite directed graph. If selected cycles use L vertices altogether, the remaining N−L vertices supply diagonal factors t.
    Weighted adjacency matrix
    An edge u→v of weight w is stored in Aᵤᵥ, so row u lists the weights of edges leaving u.
    Directed simple cycle
    A closed directed route that repeats no vertex except its starting vertex at the end. A loop has length one.
    Vertex-disjoint cycle collection
    A set of cycles sharing no vertex. Vertices not selected on a cycle contribute diagonal factors t.
    Before reading the statementOpen the geometric idea and the role of the result

    IntuitionA cycle of any length contributes one minus sign: its permutation sign and the minus signs from the selected matrix entries always multiply to −1.

    Complete manuscript statement

    Lemma II.7.1 (Directed-cycle expansion of the characteristic polynomial). Let GG be a weighted directed graph on NN vertices, with no parallel edges, adjacency matrix AA, and edge-weight function ww. Then det⁡(tIN−A)=∑𝒞(−1)|𝒞|tN−|V(𝒞)|∏e∈E(𝒞)w(e),\det(tI_N-A) =\sum_{\mathcal C} (-1)^{|\mathcal C|} t^{N-|V(\mathcal C)|} \prod_{e\in E(\mathcal C)}w(e), where the sum ranges over all collections 𝒞\mathcal C of pairwise vertex-disjoint directed cycles in GG, including the empty collection. Here V(𝒞)V(\mathcal C) and E(𝒞)E(\mathcal C) are the unions of the vertices and edges of the selected cycles. Loops are allowed, and the empty product is one.

    Complete proofOpen the manuscript proof and its guided explanation

    Proof. Expand the determinant by the Leibniz formula. Decompose each permutation into its nontrivial cycles and fixed points. At a fixed point uu, expand the factor t−Auut-A_{uu}: choosing −Auu-A_{uu} selects the loop at uu, whereas choosing tt leaves uu outside the selected cycles. Thus each resulting monomial is determined by the nontrivial permutation cycles together with a chosen subset of loops at the remaining fixed points; these form a collection of pairwise vertex-disjoint directed cycles. Every vertex not in that collection contributes tt. A directed cycle u1→u2→⋯→uℓ→u1u_1\to u_2\to\cdots\to u_\ell\to u_1 has permutation sign (−1)ℓ−1(-1)^{\ell-1}, while selecting the −A-A entry at its ℓ\ell vertices contributes (−1)ℓ(-1)^\ell. Its net sign is therefore −1-1, independently of ℓ\ell. For ℓ=1\ell=1, this says precisely that selecting the loop chooses −Auu-A_{uu} rather than tt from t−Auut-A_{uu}. Thus a collection of |𝒞||\mathcal C| cycles has sign (−1)|𝒞|(-1)^{|\mathcal C|}, contributes the product of its edge weights, and leaves N−|V(𝒞)|N-|V(\mathcal C)| diagonal factors tt. Summing over all collections, including the empty one, gives the formula. ◻

    3. 3

  2. II.7.2
    Lemma II.7.2

    Cycle collections in the sparse realization graph

    Proves that the designed graph has no cycles beyond the local q-cycles and one global s-cycle.

    Definitions and notation used here4 entries to consult as needed
    Block path
    A directed path in which every nonterminal vertex has one outgoing edge, of weight one.
    Within-block return edge
    The terminal edge of weight β that returns to the first vertex of the same block.
    Inter-block edge
    The terminal edge of weight α that enters the next block.
    Subdivision vertex
    A new vertex inserted on one inter-block edge to lengthen the cycle through all blocks without adding a choice.
    Before reading the statementOpen the geometric idea and the role of the result

    IntuitionAt every block terminal, a simple cycle uses either the within-block return edge and closes in that block, or the inter-block edge and continues through all blocks.

    Complete manuscript statement

    Lemma II.7.2 (Directed cycles in the realization digraph). Let dd and qq be positive integers. For each j∈ℤ/dℤj\in\mathbb Z/d\mathbb Z, let Bj={vj,0,…,vj,q−1}B_j=\{v_{j,0},\ldots,v_{j,q-1}\} be a set of vertices, with the sets BjB_j pairwise disjoint, and put in the directed path vj,0→vj,1→⋯→vj,q−1.v_{j,0}\longrightarrow v_{j,1}\longrightarrow\cdots \longrightarrow v_{j,q-1}. From each terminal vertex vj,q−1v_{j,q-1} add a return edge to vj,0v_{j,0} and an inter-block edge to a specified vertex vj+1,aj+1v_{j+1,a_{j+1}} of the next block, where 0≤aj+1<q0\leq a_{j+1}<q. At most one inter-block edge may instead be replaced by a directed path through new vertices, each of which has its unique outgoing edge to the next vertex of that path. When d=1d=1 and a0=0a_0=0, require this inter-block connection to be subdivided, so that it is distinct from the return edge.

    Then every directed simple cycle is either the within-block qq-cycle Cj:vj,0→⋯→vj,q−1→vj,0C_j:v_{j,0}\to\cdots\to v_{j,q-1}\to v_{j,0} for some jj, or the unique directed cycle C*C_* containing all dd inter-block connections. The cycle C*C_* meets every CjC_j at the terminal vertex of block jj. Consequently the collections of pairwise vertex-disjoint directed cycles are exactly

    1. an arbitrary subset of {C0,…,Cd−1}\{C_0,\ldots,C_{d-1}\}, or

    2. the singleton collection {C*}\{C_*\}.

    Complete proofOpen the manuscript proof and its guided explanation

    Proof. A directed cycle using no inter-block edge is confined to one block. The directed path forces it to the terminal vertex, and the only edge that both remains in that block and closes a cycle is the return edge. Hence it is CjC_j.

    Now let a simple cycle use an inter-block connection into block jj. From its entry vertex vj,ajv_{j,a_j}, the directed suffix is forced until the terminal vertex. If the cycle then used the return edge, the path from vj,0v_{j,0} would reach the already visited entry vertex before the cycle could close: when aj=0a_j=0, the return edge itself immediately repeats that entry vertex, and when aj>0a_j>0, the forced path from vj,0v_{j,0} reaches vj,ajv_{j,a_j} first. Either case contradicts simplicity. Thus the cycle must use the inter-block connection at every terminal it reaches. These connections advance cyclically through all dd blocks, so they determine the unique cycle C*C_*. Any subdivision vertices lie on that cycle and create no additional choice.

    The cycle C*C_* contains the terminal vertex of every block, which also belongs to CjC_j. Thus C*C_* is not vertex-disjoint from any CjC_j, whereas the cycles CjC_j in distinct blocks are pairwise vertex-disjoint. This is the asserted classification. ◻

    Guided proof
    1. 1
      No inter-block edge means one block cycle
      The directed path reaches the terminal, whose only within-block exit is the return edge. The entire q-cycle is forced.
    2. 2
      The outgoing arcs determine the path
      After an inter-block edge enters a block, each intermediate vertex has one outgoing edge, so the path to that block’s terminal is uniquely determined.
    3. 3
      A later local return would repeat a vertex
      The local path would reach the already visited entry point before the original cycle closed, contradicting simplicity.
    4. 4
      All later exits are inter-block
      Repeating the same reasoning moves through all d blocks and creates the unique cycle containing every inter-block edge.
    5. 5
      Classify disjoint collections
      Distinct local cycles live in disjoint blocks. The global cycle visits every terminal, so it intersects every local cycle.

    ConclusionThe allowed collections are exactly an arbitrary subset of local cycles or the singleton global cycle.

Two cases

The cases s≤dq and s>dq

The target polynomial contains local cycles of length q and one global cycle of length s. With d local blocks, the unmodified cycle containing all inter-block edges has length dq. If s<dq, some inter-block edges enter after the first vertex of a block and shorten this cycle. If s=dq, every inter-block edge enters at the first vertex and no shortening is required. If s>dq, subdividing one inter-block edge lengthens the cycle. The local q-cycles retain their length in every case.

The construction uses N₀=max(dq,s) states before padding. Farey arithmetic gives both dq≤n and s≤n, so the construction never uses more than n states. Absorbing-state padding then reaches exactly order n when necessary.

The limiting cases are included explicitly. If q=1, the within-block cycles are loops. If d=1, then s>dq. If s=dq, coprimality of the consecutive Farey denominators forces q=1. Finally, the cases α=0 and α=1 follow by polynomial continuity from 0<α<1.

XI.B

Sparse stochastic realization and attainment

The theorem handles both cases with an explicit weighted digraph, verifies every row sum, treats α=0 and α=1 by polynomial continuity, and computes the exact reduced Ito polynomial. The following corollary applies it to the compact curve defined in Topic IX.

    Corollary II.7.4Previously known

    Stochastic realization of the compact candidate curve

    Proves Γf,g(n)⊆Θₙ, including the Farey endpoints and the exceptional order-three real segment.

    Before reading the statementOpen the geometric idea and the role of the result

    IntuitionFor an interior argument, Topic IX supplies complementary coefficients and a nonzero root of the Ito polynomial. Theorem II.7.3 realizes that root. Its endpoint parameters realize the adjacent roots of unity, and the same order-three family covers the exceptional real segment.

    Complete manuscript statement
