Topic XII · Manuscript pages 94–101

Farey refinement and monotonicity of the candidate radius

Why is Kₙ(θ) nondecreasing with respect to n for 0≤θ≤π, and why does conjugation give the reflected lower-half comparison?

Topic orientationWhat this chapter proves and why it comes here

Mediant insertion refines one Farey interval into two subintervals. A log-line comparison, the case where the factor count increases, and an exhaustive Farey classification compare the corresponding candidate radii.

The upper-half inequality Kₙ₋₁(θ)≤Kₙ(θ), together with its conjugate lower-half comparison, is the global arithmetic step that lets the final proof pass from radial maxima first appearing at one order to all stochastic eigenvalues by induction.

Reading convention. Essential definitions appear before the first statement that needs them. Complete manuscript proofs are closed by default. When a result has an added line-by-line explanation, it appears inside the same disclosure as the proof.

Standard background, stated with sources

  • Classical Farey refinement: If an old interval is divided when passing from order n−1 to order n, the newly inserted fraction is its mediant.
  • Strict convexity of −log cos: on every interval where a line has a positive radial intersection, its log-radius has second derivative sec²>0. The two-line derivation is reproduced before the first lemma in its exact proof.
  • Determinants in the real plane: supply the signed side test; multiplication by a nonzero complex number scales every real determinant by its positive squared modulus.
  • Monotone zero comparison: if a strictly increasing function is nonpositive at a and vanishes at b, then a≤b.

Definitions before the comparisons

Farey data, factor count, and candidate radius

Topic XII presents manuscript §II.8. These conventions are used throughout the chapter.

Farey intervals and rays. Fn is the Farey sequence of order n, Fn+=Fn∩[0,1/2], and ℛθ={teiθ:t≥0} is the ray of angle θ. The endpoints of an interval may be labelled p/q and r/s so that q<s; this denominator-based labelling need not be their left-to-right order. The absolute definitions of A and B cover both orientations.

Factor count and determinant. For an endpoint denominator q, Dn(q)=⌊n/q⌋. Throughout Topic XII, det(z,w)=Im(z̄w) under the identification ℂ≃ℝ².

Candidate radius. If x=θ/(2π) lies in an open interval between consecutive fractions of Fn+, then Kn(θ) is Topic IX’s unique radius satisfying the scalar equation. At every Farey endpoint below π set Kn(θ)=1, and separately set Kn(π)=1. The separate definition at θ=π records the order-three jump: the nonreal arc tends to radius 1/2 as θ↑π, while the endpoint −1 has radius 1.

Logical status. The notation Kn denotes the candidate selected by the scalar equation. Topic XIII, not the notation itself, proves that this value equals the maximum modulus attained by Θn on the same ray.

Preliminary formulas

Line-intersection and convexity formulas

After replacing ρ by R=1/ρ, the old candidate gives a line through transformed endpoints with positive-real intersection 1. The two lemmas compare the corresponding new line or scalar residual.

A notational warning. In Lemma II.8.1, d is the denominator in the endpoint c/d, while the old factor count is called m. In Lemma II.8.2, the old and new factor counts are called m and M=m+1. These are roles, not interchangeable symbols.

The log-radial function is explicit. Let L be a line not through the origin. On an interval of directions for which the ray {reiφ:r>0} meets L, call that intersection radius r(φ) and put ℓ(φ)=log r(φ). For constants c and φ0, ℓ(φ)=c−log cos(φ−φ0) and ℓ″(φ)=sec²(φ−φ0)>0 on the chosen interval, where |φ−φ0|<π/2. Here sec u=1/cos u. This is equation (II.8.1), and it proves that ℓ′ is strictly increasing on the interval used below.

The sign convention is fixed algebraically. With det(z,w)=Im(z̄w), take X=PeiA and Y=Qe−iC, with P,Q,A,C>0 and A+C<π, define ΔX,Y(r)=det(Y−X,r−X)=r(P sin A+Q sin C)−PQ sin(A+C).

Its positive-real intercept is I(P,Q)=PQ sin(A+C)/(P sin A+Q sin C), and therefore I(P,Q)>1 exactly when ΔX,Y(1)<0. These are equations (II.8.2)–(II.8.4). No visual “left side/right side” convention enters the proof.

Why moving either endpoint outward helpsOpen the two derivative signs

Holding the angles fixed, direct differentiation gives ∂I/∂P=Q² sin(A+C) sin C/(P sin A+Q sin C)²>0 and the symmetric formula ∂I/∂Q=P² sin(A+C) sin A/(P sin A+Q sin C)²>0. Every sine and denominator in these formulas is positive under the displayed angle assumptions.

Four-result proof sequence

From local Farey comparisons to global monotonicity

Lemmas II.8.1 and II.8.2 treat the two strict ways the formula at an interior argument can change. Lemma II.8.3 proves that these, the unchanged case, and the endpoint cases exhaust the refinement. Theorem II.8.4 then evaluates the new scalar residual at the old candidate and compares its unique zero.

    Guided proof
    1. 1
      Define the old comparison line and its log-radial function
      R is the reciprocal of the old candidate radius. The vector equation writes 1 as a strict convex combination of U and V, so 1 lies in the relative interior of their segment. Consequently ℓ(0)=0, while ℓ records the logarithms of the endpoint moduli.

      Check. The coefficients α and β lie strictly between 0 and 1 because the prescribed argument lies in the open Farey interval.

    2. 2
      Treat the left subinterval when b<d and b>1
      Coprimality of the neighbouring denominators prevents b from dividing d; hence the factor count does not change. With the selected root U1/m=Rb/meiA/m, the new endpoint is W=VU1/m. Integrating the strictly increasing derivative ℓ′ over two scaled angular intervals proves that |W| exceeds the old line’s radius in the direction of W.
    3. 3
      Prove ΔU,W(1)<0
      Write W=γV* with γ>1 and V* on the old segment. Collinearity cancels one determinant term, leaving −(γ−1)Rbsin A<0. The determinant sign criterion therefore puts the new intercept strictly beyond 1.
    4. 4
      Treat the right subinterval
      Here A>mB. Both new endpoints are obtained by multiplying points of the old line by Vm. Strict increase of ℓ′ again produces a scaling γ<1 on the relevant old-line point. Multiplication by a nonzero complex number scales real determinants by its positive squared modulus, so it cannot reverse the sign.
    5. 5
      Handle b=1 by the line-intersection formula
      When b=1, the factor count changes from n−1 to n, so the preceding unchanged-factor-count argument does not apply. The proof instead writes the radial function of the chord from 1 to a root of unity explicitly and differentiates it with respect to the endpoint angle. The derivative is negative, so the smaller new endpoint angle produces the larger radius.
    6. 6
      Reduce d<b by x↦1−x and conjugation
      If d<b, complex conjugation and reduction modulo one replace the interval by 1−c/d<1−a/b. The smaller denominator is then on the left, while moduli, scalar equations, and positive-real intersection coordinates are preserved.
    7. 7
      Handle Farey endpoint angles
      The strict comparison concerns angles whose normalized values lie in the two open subintervals. At either inherited Farey endpoint both candidate radii are defined to be 1, so equality there is part of the definition rather than a limiting claim.
