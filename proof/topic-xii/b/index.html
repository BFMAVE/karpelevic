Topic XII · Part B · Manuscript pages 100–101

Farey refinement and monotonicity of the candidate radius — monotonicity of the candidate radius

Why is Kₙ(θ) nondecreasing with respect to the order n for every angle θ?

Topic orientationWhat this chapter proves and why it comes here

Mediant insertion refines one Farey interval into two subintervals. A log-line comparison, the case where the factor count increases, and an exhaustive Farey classification compare the corresponding candidate radii.

The pointwise inequality Kₙ₋₁(θ)≤Kₙ(θ) is the global arithmetic step that lets the final proof pass from radial maxima first appearing at one order to all stochastic eigenvalues by induction.

Standard background, stated with sources

  • Classical Farey refinement: a newly admitted reduced fraction has denominator exactly n, and an interval between unimodular neighbours is divided at their mediant.
  • Monotone zero comparison: if a strictly increasing function is nonpositive at a and vanishes at b, then a≤b.

Proved on this page

Lemma II.8.3 proves that the Farey refinement cases are exhaustive. Theorem II.8.4 applies one scalar sign test in every case and obtains the pointwise inequality Kn−1(θ)≤Kn(θ).

Notation recalled from Part A

The candidate radius Kₙ

Part A states the full definition before either comparison lemma.

On an open Farey-interval ray, Kn(θ) is Topic IX’s unique scalar equality radius. It equals 1 at Farey endpoints and, by the separate convention in Part A, at θ=π.

The endpoints of an interval may be labelled p/q and r/s so that q<s; this denominator-based labelling need not be their left-to-right order. The absolute definitions of A and B cover both orientations.

Part B · Exhaustion and assembly

Proof that Kₙ₋₁(θ)≤Kₙ(θ)

The case split first proves that every ray is accounted for. The theorem then evaluates the new scalar residual at the old candidate and reads the sign against a unique zero.

  1. II.8.3
    Lemma II.8.3

    Classification of the changes from Fₙ₋₁⁺ to Fₙ⁺

    Prove that inherited endpoints, new endpoints, unchanged Farey intervals, and intervals divided by a mediant exhaust the comparison between consecutive orders.

    Definitions and notation used here2 entries to consult as needed
    New fraction set Fₙ⁺∖Fₙ₋₁⁺
    These are precisely the reduced upper-half Farey fractions first admitted at order n. Each has reduced denominator exactly n.
    Unimodular basis of ℤ²
    Because bc−ad=1, the vectors (b,a) and (d,c) form a basis of ℤ². A primitive vector (n,h) whose slope lies strictly between theirs has positive integer coordinates in this basis.
    Before reading the statementOpen the geometric idea and the role of the result

    IntuitionA single step from denominator bound n−1 to n can admit only fractions whose reduced denominator is exactly n. Between old neighbours the unimodular-basis decomposition forces such a fraction, if present, to be their mediant. Everything else is either an endpoint or remains inside an unchanged interval.

    Complete manuscript statement

    Lemma II.8.3 (Exhaustive order-refinement cases). Let n≥4n\ge4 and let θ/(2π)∈[0,1/2]\theta/(2\pi)\in[0,1/2]. Comparing Fn−1+F_{n-1}^+ with Fn+F_n^+, exactly one of the following alternatives applies.

    1. The ray is an inherited Farey endpoint; both candidate radii are defined to be 11.

    2. The ray is a newly inserted endpoint of denominator nn; the order nn candidate radius is 11, while the order n−1n-1 ray is interior to one old interval.

    3. The ray is interior to an old interval which is not split at this order. Then the endpoint pair is unchanged, and for the smaller denominator qq the factor count changes from ⌊(n−1)/q⌋\lfloor(n-1)/q\rfloor to ⌊n/q⌋\lfloor n/q\rfloor, hence either stays the same or increases by exactly one.

    4. The ray is interior to one of the two intervals obtained by inserting the mediant (a+c)/(b+d)(a+c)/(b+d) between consecutive old endpoints a/b<c/da/b<c/d; in this case b+d=nb+d=n.

    There are no further cases.

    Complete proofOpen the manuscript proof and its guided explanation

    Proof. The only fractions which can appear in FnF_n but not in Fn−1F_{n-1} have reduced denominator exactly nn. If such a fraction h/nh/n is inserted between consecutive old endpoints a/b<c/da/b<c/d, the Farey adjacency criterion Lemma II.2.1 gives bc−ad=1bc-ad=1 and b+d≥nb+d\ge n. Thus the vectors (b,a)(b,a) and (d,c)(d,c) form a unimodular basis of ℤ2\mathbb Z^2. Since h/nh/n lies strictly between the endpoints, its coordinates in this basis are the positive integers m=cn−dh,ℓ=bh−an,m=cn-dh, \qquad \ell=bh-an, and therefore (n,h)=m(b,a)+ℓ(d,c)(n,h)=m(b,a)+\ell(d,c) and n=mb+ℓd≥b+dn=mb+\ell d\ge b+d. Hence b+d=nb+d=n, m=ℓ=1m=\ell=1, and h/n=(a+c)/(b+d)h/n=(a+c)/(b+d). This proves that every new interior split is exactly the mediant case and that no other old interval is split.

    If θ2π∈Fn+\Fn−1+,\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\in F_n^+\setminus F_{n-1}^+, then case (ii) applies. If θ/(2π)∈Fn−1+\theta/(2\pi)\in F_{n-1}^+, then case (i) applies. Otherwise the ray is interior to an old interval. When that old interval is split, case (iv) applies; when it is not split, the endpoint pair is unchanged. Increasing the order from n−1n-1 to nn can change ⌊n/q⌋\lfloor n/q\rfloor by at most one, so the unchanged-interval alternatives in case (iii) are exhaustive. The upper-half restriction merely discards the reflected lower-half copies and does not introduce another case. ◻

    Guided proof
    1. 1
      Identify what can be new
      Every fraction of denominator at most n−1 was already eligible. Therefore a point of Fn\Fn−1 has reduced denominator exactly n.
    2. 2
      Place a new fraction between old neighbours
      For old neighbours a/b<c/d, Farey adjacency gives bc−ad=1. The resulting unimodular basis writes the primitive vector (n,h) of an inserted fraction as m(b,a)+ℓ(d,c) with positive integers m,ℓ.
    3. 3
      Compare n≥b+d with b+d≥n
      The decomposition gives n=mb+ℓd≥b+d, while old adjacency at order n−1 gives b+d≥n. Equality holds throughout, forcing m=ℓ=1 and h/n=(a+c)/(b+d).
    4. 4
      Classify the ray itself
      The normalized angle θ/(2π) is either an old endpoint, a new endpoint, or lies inside an old interval. Such an interval is either divided by the unique mediant or unchanged.
    5. 5
      Determine whether Dₙ(q) changes
      For a fixed smaller denominator q, the difference Dn(q)−Dn−1(q) is either zero or one. This completes case (iii).

    ConclusionThe two local comparisons from Part A cover every open-ray change; endpoint rays require only their defining value 1.

    This classification is derived from the classical Farey adjacency and mediant properties; the proof records explicitly why these four cases are exhaustive for the radius comparison.

  2. II.8.4
    Theorem II.8.4

    Monotonicity of Kₙ(θ) with respect to n

    Assemble all local comparisons into the global inequality Kₙ₋₁(θ)≤Kₙ(θ) on every ray.

    Definitions and notation used here2 entries to consult as needed
    Candidate radius Kₙ(θ)
    On a ray whose normalized angle lies in an open Farey interval, it is the unique scalar equality radius from Topic IX. At every Farey endpoint below π, and separately at θ=π, it is defined to be 1.
    Order-n scalar residual Fₙ,θ(t)
    The left side of the order-n scalar radius equation minus its right side, evaluated at a test radius t. Formula (II.8.22) shows that its derivative is positive for t>0.
    Open an example
    If Fn,θ(0.8)<0 and its unique zero is 0.9, strict increase certifies 0.8<0.9 without solving the equation again.
    Before reading the statementOpen the geometric idea and the role of the result

    IntuitionInstead of comparing two complicated curves directly, insert the old radius into the new scalar equation. The exhaustive four-case classification shows that the new residual there is always nonpositive. Because the new residual increases strictly and crosses zero exactly once, its zero must lie at or to the right of the old radius.

    Complete manuscript statement

    Theorem II.8.4 (Order monotonicity of the candidate radius). With this definition, Kn−1(θ)≤Kn(θ)\begin{equation} \label{karp:eq:candidate-nesting} K_{n-1}(\theta)\leq K_n(\theta) \end{equation}(II.8.20) for every 0≤θ≤π0\leq\theta\leq\pi and every n≥4n\geq4; the lower-half-plane version follows by conjugation.

    Complete proofOpen the manuscript proof and its guided explanation

    Proof. Fix first an angle θ\theta which is interior to an interval of both Fn−1+F_{n-1}^+ and Fn+F_n^+. Label the endpoints of its order-nn interval as p/q,r/sp/q,r/s with q<sq<s. This is a denominator-based labeling, not necessarily their left-to-right order. Put D=⌊nq⌋,D=\left\lfloor \frac nq\right\rfloor, and let A,BA,B be the positive quantities in equation (II.2.6). Define the order-nn scalar residual ℱn,θ(t):=ts/Dsin⁡A+tqsin⁡B−sin⁡(A+B),t>0.\begin{equation} \label{karp:eq:candidate-scalar-defect} \mathcal F_{n,\theta}(t) :=t^{s/D}\sin A+t^q\sin B-\sin(A+B), \qquad t>0. \end{equation}(II.8.21) Its derivative has the fixed sign ℱn,θ′(t)=sDts/D−1sin⁡A+qtq−1sin⁡B>0.\begin{equation} \label{karp:eq:candidate-defect-derivative} \mathcal F_{n,\theta}'(t) =\frac{s}{D}t^{s/D-1}\sin A +qt^{q-1}\sin B>0. \end{equation}(II.8.22) By Proposition II.2.3, Kn(θ)K_n(\theta) is the unique zero of ℱn,θ\mathcal F_{n,\theta}.

    Set ρ−=Kn−1(θ),R=ρ−−1.\rho_-=K_{n-1}(\theta), \qquad R=\rho_-^{-1}. By Lemma II.8.3, there are three interior-ray possibilities.

    If the Farey interval and its factor count are unchanged, then its scalar equation is unchanged and ℱn,θ(ρ−)=0.\mathcal F_{n,\theta}(\rho_-)=0. If the interval is unchanged but its factor count increases, then equation (II.8.18) gives ℱn,θ(ρ−)<0.\mathcal F_{n,\theta}(\rho_-)<0.

    It remains to consider an interval created by inserting a new denominator-nn mediant. The two reciprocal-coordinate endpoints of the new line at ρ−\rho_- are, after choosing their common orientation, RqeiA,Rs/De−iB.R^q e^{iA}, \qquad R^{s/D}e^{-iB}. Its positive-real intercept is IR=Rq+s/Dsin⁡(A+B)Rqsin⁡A+Rs/Dsin⁡B.I_R= \frac{R^{q+s/D}\sin(A+B)} {R^q\sin A+R^{s/D}\sin B}. Direct subtraction, without a side convention, gives IR−1=Rq+s/D(−ℱn,θ(ρ−))Rqsin⁡A+Rs/Dsin⁡B.\begin{equation} \label{karp:eq:intercept-defect-identity} I_R-1 = \frac{R^{q+s/D} \bigl(-\mathcal F_{n,\theta}(\rho_-)\bigr)} {R^q\sin A+R^{s/D}\sin B}. \end{equation}(II.8.23) By Lemma II.8.1, IR>1I_R>1; every other factor in equation (II.8.23) is positive. Hence ℱn,θ(ρ−)<0.\mathcal F_{n,\theta}(\rho_-)<0. Thus in all three cases ℱn,θ(ρ−)≤0.\mathcal F_{n,\theta}(\rho_-)\leq0. Together with equation (II.8.22) and ℱn,θ(Kn(θ))=0\mathcal F_{n,\theta}(K_n(\theta))=0, this proves Kn−1(θ)=ρ−≤Kn(θ)K_{n-1}(\theta)=\rho_-\leq K_n(\theta) on every ray interior to both Farey decompositions. The inequality is strict whenever the interval is split or its factor count increases.

    Now let θ/(2π)\theta/(2\pi) be a newly inserted fraction. It is interior to one order-(n−1)(n-1) interval, so Kn−1(θ)<1K_{n-1}(\theta)<1 by Proposition II.2.3, whereas Kn(θ)=1K_n(\theta)=1 by the endpoint definition. At every inherited Farey endpoint below π\pi, both values are 11. At θ=0\theta=0 the same is true, and at θ=π\theta=\pi both sides are 11 by equation (II.8.19). The latter separate clause is essential for n=4n=4, because lim⁡θ↑πK3(θ)=1/2\lim_{\theta\uparrow\pi}K_3(\theta)=1/2 although K3(π)=1K_3(\pi)=1. This proves equation (II.8.20) everywhere. Conjugation gives the lower-half-plane statement. ◻

    Guided proof
    1. 1
      Define Fₙ,θ and prove it is strictly increasing
      For a fixed open ray, define Fn,θ(t) from the order-n scalar equation. The two sine coefficients and both exponents are positive, so the displayed derivative is positive for every t>0. Topic IX identifies Kn(θ) as its unique zero.
    2. 2
      Evaluate the unchanged equation at the old radius
      If neither the Farey interval nor its factor count changes, the scalar equation is literally the same. Hence the new residual at ρ−=Kn−1(θ) equals zero.
    3. 3
      Use Lemma II.8.2 when the factor count increases
      If the interval stays fixed but its factor count increases, Lemma II.8.2 gives the strict sign Fn,θ(ρ−)<0.
    4. 4
      Use Lemma II.8.1 after a mediant insertion
      Lemma II.8.1 makes the new reciprocal-coordinate line’s intercept IR exceed 1. Identity (II.8.23) expresses IR−1 as a positive factor times −Fn,θ(ρ−), so the residual is again strictly negative.
    5. 5
      Use strict monotonicity to compare the two zeros
      All interior cases give Fn,θ(ρ−)≤0. Strict increase and Fn,θ(Kn)=0 force ρ−≤Kn. The inequality is strict exactly after a mediant insertion or a factor-count increase.
    6. 6
      Handle Farey endpoint angles
      At a new endpoint the new value is 1 while the old ray was interior and had radius below 1. At an inherited endpoint both values are 1. The separate convention Kn(π)=1 handles the order-three terminal jump when comparing orders three and four.
    7. 7
      Reflect to the lower half-plane
      Complex conjugation preserves every modulus and completes the full-circle statement.

    ConclusionFor every angle, Kn−1(θ)≤Kn(θ). Topic XIII uses this pointwise inequality when a stochastic extremum is inherited from the preceding order.
