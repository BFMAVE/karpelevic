Topic XIII · Manuscript pages 101–105

The Karpelevič theorem in Ito’s formulation

How do the upper bound, attainment, and nesting assemble into the complete stochastic eigenvalue region?

Topic orientationWhat this chapter proves and why it comes here

The small orders are proved directly. For every later order, each ray is either genuinely new or inherited from the preceding order; the sharp inequality and nesting identify the same candidate radius in both cases.

Continuity of the radial graph, conjugation, star-shapedness with respect to the origin, and the unit-circle classification then identify the concatenated candidate curves with the full topological boundary. This is where the classical theorem is finally stated and proved.

Structure of the final proof

Three tasks remain—and only three

Every deep geometric and analytic estimate has already been proved. This chapter supplies one topological lemma, checks the base orders, and runs one two-branch induction.

  1. 1

    Topology

    Show that knowing one continuous maximum on every ray identifies the actual boundary.

  2. 2

    Base cases

    Compute orders one through three directly, without appealing to the induction.

  3. 3

    Induction

    On each ray, separate a genuinely new extremum from an inherited one and prove Rn=Kn in both branches.

I · The topological lemma

Outer on every ray becomes boundary everywhere

Radial maximality is a one-dimensional statement. This lemma adds exactly the continuity needed to recover the two-dimensional topological boundary.

  1. II.9.1
    Lemma II.9.1Classical result

    Compact star-shaped sets with continuous radial function

    Prove the final topological step: the outer point on every ray forms the complete topological boundary, not merely a collection of radial maxima.

    Definitions and notation used here7 entries to consult as needed
    Star-shaped with respect to 0
    A set S is star-shaped with respect to 0 when z∈S and 0≤t≤1 imply tz∈S. Every attained point brings the entire segment from 0 to that point with it.
    Open an example
    A closed disk centred at 0 is star-shaped with respect to 0. An annulus is not, because points near its outer circle do not bring the missing segment near 0.
    Radial maximum rₛ(φ)
    The largest radius r for which reiφ lies in S. Compactness makes the maximum attainable rather than merely a supremum.
    Topological boundary ∂S
    The points for which every sufficiently small open disk meets both S and its complement. Because S is compact and therefore closed, this is also S minus its Euclidean interior in the whole plane ℂ≅ℝ²—not a relative interior inside a line or curve.
    Continuous on the circle
    Nearby directions have nearby radial maxima, including across the identification of the angles −π and π. This prevents cracks or jumps between neighbouring ray segments.
    Positive radial maximum
    The assumption rS(φ)>0 for every direction. By continuity on the compact circle it then has a positive minimum, so S contains a small disk about the origin.
    Euclidean open disk
    For a centre z and radius ε>0, the points w satisfying |w−z|<ε. A point is interior precisely when some such disk around it lies in the set.
    Extreme value theorem
    A continuous real-valued function on a compact set attains both a minimum and a maximum. Applied to the circle, it turns pointwise positivity of rS into one uniform positive lower bound.
    Before reading the statementOpen the geometric idea and the role of the result

    IntuitionStar-shapedness with respect to 0 settles each individual ray, but topology also sees nearby directions. Continuity supplies that transverse control: a point strictly below the graph has room both radially and angularly, so it lies inside a small disk contained in S. An outer endpoint has no such disk because moving slightly farther along its ray leaves S.

    Complete manuscript statement

    Lemma II.9.1 (Compact star-shaped sets with continuous radial function). Let S⊂ℂS\subset\mathbb C be compact and star-shaped with respect to the origin, and suppose that rS(ϕ):=max⁡{r≥0:reiϕ∈S}r_S(\phi):=\max\{r\geq0:re^{i\phi}\in S\} is positive and continuous on the circle. Then S={trS(ϕ)eiϕ:0≤t≤1,−π<ϕ≤π},∂S={rS(ϕ)eiϕ:−π<ϕ≤π}.\begin{equation} \label{karp:eq:compact-radial-set} S=\{t\,r_S(\phi)e^{i\phi}:0\leq t\leq1,\ -\pi<\phi\leq\pi\}, \qquad \partial S=\{r_S(\phi)e^{i\phi}:-\pi<\phi\leq\pi\}. \end{equation}(II.9.1)

    Complete proofOpen the manuscript proof and its guided explanation

    Proof. Compactness makes each maximum attainable, and star-shapedness with respect to the origin then says that the intersection of SS with the ray of angle ϕ\phi is exactly {reiϕ:0≤r≤rS(ϕ)}\{re^{i\phi}:0\leq r\leq r_S(\phi)\}. Taking the union over ϕ\phi gives the first formula. A point rS(ϕ)eiϕr_S(\phi)e^{i\phi} cannot be interior, since a sufficiently small outward radial displacement would contradict maximality.

    Conversely, let z=reiϕz=re^{i\phi} with 0<r<rS(ϕ)0<r<r_S(\phi). Choose η>0\eta>0 with r+3η<rS(ϕ)r+3\eta<r_S(\phi). By continuity, rS(ψ)>r+2ηr_S(\psi)>r+2\eta for all ψ\psi in some neighborhood of ϕ\phi. A sufficiently small Euclidean ball about zz consists of points ww with arg⁡w\arg w in that neighborhood and |w|<r+η|w|<r+\eta; the ray description then puts the whole ball in SS. Thus every nonzero point strictly below the radial graph is interior. Finally, positivity and compactness of the circle give c:=min⁡ϕrS(ϕ)>0c:=\min_\phi r_S(\phi)>0, so the disk |z|<c|z|<c lies in SS and 00 is interior. No further boundary points remain. ◻

    Guided proof
    1. 1
      Describe every ray section exactly
      Compactness attains rS(φ), and star-shapedness with respect to 0 contains every smaller radius. Maximality excludes every larger radius. Thus the intersection with one ray is precisely the closed segment from 0 to the displayed outer point.
    2. 2
      Exclude an outer point from the interior
      Every open disk around rS(φ)eiφ contains a point obtained by moving a little farther outward on the same ray. That point contradicts maximality and lies outside S.
    3. 3
      Give a strictly shorter point radial room
      For z=reiφ with 0<r<rS(φ), choose η>0 so that r+3η<rS(φ). The extra factors of η create a margin that survives small changes of direction and small Euclidean displacement.
    4. 4
      Use continuity for angular room
      For ψ near φ, continuity gives rS(ψ)>r+2η. A sufficiently small Euclidean disk about z consists of points with such nearby arguments and modulus below r+η, hence lies entirely in S.
    5. 5
      Handle the origin
      A positive continuous function on a compact circle has a positive minimum c. Every direction contains its radial segment to at least c, so the disk |z|<c lies in S and 0 is interior.
    6. 6
      Exhaust the boundary
      Every strictly shorter point is interior; every radial maximum is not interior; every point of S lies on one of the ray segments. No further boundary point remains.

    ConclusionOnce the actual radius is known continuously on every ray, the outer radial graph is automatically the full topological boundary and its radial hull is the whole set.

    Classical result. This is a standard polar-coordinate fact for compact star-shaped planar sets. The manuscript states and proves the precise version needed here.

    • R. Schneider, Convex Bodies: The Brunn–Minkowski Theory, expanded ed., Cambridge University Press (2014): Chapter 1, §§1.1, 1.3, 1.6, 1.7, and Chapter 2, §§2.1, 2.4.

II · The induction base

The exceptional small orders are computed directly

Order three is not a cosmetic special case: its last nonreal arc ends at −1/2 while the outer point of the negative-real ray is −1.

  1. II.9.2
    Proposition II.9.2Previously known

    Orders one, two, and three

    Prove the induction base directly, including the exceptional real segment that makes order three unlike every later order.

    Definitions and notation used here10 entries to consult as needed
    Actual outer radius Rₙ(θ)
    The largest radius ρ for which ρeiθ belongs to the stochastic eigenvalue region Θn. Topic VIII proves that this maximum is attained.
    Candidate outer radius Kₙ(θ)
    Topic XII's notation for the modulus constructed in Topic IX on an open Farey interval, with value 1 at ordinary Farey endpoints. It remains a candidate until the induction below identifies it with Rn.
    Trace tr(A)
    The sum of the diagonal entries of A. It also equals the sum of the eigenvalues, counted with algebraic multiplicity.
    Two elementary spectral facts used here
    A row-stochastic matrix has eigenvalue 1 because it fixes the all-ones column vector. A real matrix has its nonreal eigenvalues in conjugate pairs, so an order-three spectrum containing λ=x+iy also contains λ̄=x−iy.
    Trace of A²
    It equals the sum of the squares of the eigenvalues, again with algebraic multiplicity. For the spectrum 1,λ,λ̄ this gives tr(A²)=1+2(x²−y²).
    Cauchy–Schwarz in the form used
    For three real numbers u1,u2,u3, one has (u1+u2+u3)²≤3(u1²+u2²+u3²). The proof applies this to the three diagonal entries.
    Cyclic permutation matrix C₃
    The row-stochastic matrix that sends state 1 to 2, 2 to 3, and 3 back to 1. Its eigenvalues are 1, ω, and ω̄, where ω=e2πi/3.
    Convex hull conv{1,ω,ω̄}
    The filled triangle consisting of all nonnegative weighted averages of the three displayed vertices with weights summing to one.
    Exceptional interval
    The last upper Farey interval [1/3,1/2]. Its nonreal candidate curve approaches −1/2 rather than the endpoint root −1, so the real segment [−1,−1/2] must be included separately.
    Characteristic polynomial
    det(tI−A). Its roots, with multiplicity, are exactly the eigenvalues of A.
    Before reading the statementOpen the geometric idea and the role of the result

    IntuitionOrders one and two are elementary. At order three, trace inequalities trap every nonreal eigenvalue inside an equilateral triangle. Two explicit stochastic families attain the triangle’s boundary and the extra negative-real segment. Matching those pieces to the Topic IX candidate curve proves exactly the base identity the order-four induction will use.

    Complete manuscript statement

    Proposition II.9.2 (Orders one, two, and three). One has Θ1={1},Θ2=[−1,1],\Theta_1=\{1\}, \qquad \Theta_2=[-1,1], and, with ω=e2πi/3\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}, Θ3=conv⁡{1,ω,ω¯}∪[−1,−1/2].\begin{equation} \label{karp:eq:theta3} \Theta_3= \mathop{\mathrm{conv}}\{1,\omega,\overline\omega\} \cup[-1,-1/2]. \end{equation}(II.9.2)

    The chord [1,ω][1,\omega] is realized by (1−α)I+αC3(1-\alpha)I+\alpha C_3, 0≤α≤10\leq\alpha\leq1.     It remains to identify the two nonreal boundary pieces with the canonical radius K3K_3 used by the induction.     Guided proof
    1. 1
      Compute orders one and two
      The sole 1×1 row-stochastic matrix is [1]. A 2×2 row-stochastic matrix has rows (a,1−a) and (b,1−b); besides eigenvalue 1, its other eigenvalue is a−b, which runs through [−1,1].
    2. 2
      Parameterize a nonreal order-three spectrum
      A real 3×3 matrix with one nonreal eigenvalue λ=x+iy also has λ̄. Row-stochasticity supplies the third eigenvalue 1. Nonnegative diagonal entries give tr(A)=1+2x≥0, hence x≥−1/2.
    3. 3
      Derive the triangular slope bounds
      Cauchy–Schwarz gives (tr A)²≤3∑aii². Nonnegativity gives ∑aii²≤tr(A²), because the remaining terms aijaji are nonnegative. Substituting the eigenvalue expressions yields 3y²≤(1−x)², the other two sides of the triangle.
    4. 4
      Attain the first two triangular sides
      The convex matrix family (1−α)I+αC3 has eigenvalue (1−α)+αω, which runs along the chord [1,ω]. Real matrices give the conjugate chord [1,ω̄]. Star-shapedness with respect to 0 supplies every point between these boundary chords and 0.
    5. 5
      Attain the terminal arc and real segment
      Topic XI’s terminal sparse matrix has characteristic factor λ²+λ+α. For 0≤α≤1/4 its lower real root runs from −1 to −1/2. For 1/4<α≤1 the upper root has real part −1/2 and rises to ω; conjugation supplies the lower branch.
    6. 6
      Identify the first candidate curve
      On [0,1/3], the cell data reduce the scalar equation to the ray equation for the straight chord β+αω. Its argument increases strictly with α, so each open ray meets it once and Topic IX’s unique radius must be that chord radius.
    7. 7
      Identify the exceptional candidate curve
      On [1/3,1/2], the scalar equation is equivalent to λ(λ²−β)=α. Along λ=−1/2+iy, the argument decreases strictly from π to 2π/3 as y rises, so again every open ray is met exactly once. The real interval is an endpoint-ray addition, not part of that open-cell branch.
    8. 8
      Record the exact induction base
      The upper bounds and realizations coincide, and the endpoint convention gives radius 1 at 1, ω, and −1. Therefore the actual and candidate outer radii agree for every upper direction at order three, including the jump at θ=π.

    ConclusionThe theorem is completely proved for n≤3, and the exact equality R3=K3 is ready for the induction step.

III · The classical theorem

Every ray and the full boundary

The theorem is stated with its original manuscript number from Section II.3. Its complete proof appears here only after all later ingredients have been established, matching the logical rather than the printed order.

  1. II.3.1
    Theorem II.3.1Classical result

    Karpelevič theorem in Ito’s formulation

    Identify every boundary arc, the complete region star-shaped with respect to 0, and all unit-circle points for every matrix order.

    Definitions and notation used here7 entries to consult as needed
    Actual outer radius Rₙ(θ)
    The largest ρ for which ρeiθ is an eigenvalue of some n×n row-stochastic matrix. Compactness of Θn makes this largest value attainable.
    Candidate radius Kₙ(θ)
    The modulus constructed in Topic IX and compared across orders in Topic XII. Before this theorem it is known to be attained, but has not yet been proved to exclude every farther stochastic eigenvalue at an inherited argument.
    Natural order embedding
    Given an (n−1)×(n−1) row-stochastic matrix A, the block diagonal matrix A⊕[1] is row-stochastic of order n and retains every eigenvalue of A. Hence Θn−1⊆Θn.
    Non-inherited versus inherited
    An order-n radial maximum is non-inherited if it is not in Θn−1; it is inherited if it does belong to Θn−1. These alternatives are exhaustive.
    Concatenated candidate curves
    The continuous concatenation of the curves Γf,g(n) on consecutive Farey intervals, in increasing Farey order, beginning at 1 and ending at −1 in the upper half-plane.
    Radial hull of the boundary
    All points obtained by joining the origin to every boundary point: if ζ lies on the boundary, include tζ for every 0≤t≤1. The theorem proves that this radial hull is exactly Θn.
    Induction on n
    Prove the statement directly at the base orders, then assume it for n−1 and use that assumption to prove it for n.
    Before reading the statementOpen the geometric idea and the role of the result

    IntuitionFix one direction. Attainment puts the candidate no farther out than the actual maximum. If the actual maximum is new, the critical-polygon inequality identifies it with the candidate. If it is inherited, the order embedding and induction identify it with the old candidate, while the pointwise monotonicity from Topic XII and attainment squeeze the new candidate to that same number. Once this equality is known on every ray, continuity turns the radial graph into the topological boundary.

    Complete manuscript statement

    Theorem II.3.1 (Karpelevič theorem in Ito’s formulation). Let n≥3n\geq3. For every consecutive pair f<gf<g in Fn+F_n^+, let Γf,g(n)\Gamma_{f,g}^{(n)} be the candidate arc of Definition II.2.5. Then these arcs are the Karpelevič boundary arcs, and their ordered concatenation is the upper boundary of Θn\Theta_n, from 11 to −1-1. The lower boundary is its complex conjugate, and Θn={tζ:0≤t≤1,ζ∈∂Θn}.\begin{equation} \label{karp:eq:radial-hull-main} \Theta_n =\left\{t\zeta:0\leq t\leq 1, \ \zeta\in\partial\Theta_n\right\}. \end{equation}(II.3.1) The only points of Θn\Theta_n on the unit circle are roots of unity of order at most nn.

    For n=3n=3, the terminal candidate arc contains the boundary segment [−1,−1/2][-1,-1/2] before leaving the real axis. For n=1n=1, one has Θ1={1}\Theta_1=\{1\}; for n=2n=2, one has Θ2=[−1,1]\Theta_2=[-1,1].

    Complete proofOpen the manuscript proof and its guided explanation
