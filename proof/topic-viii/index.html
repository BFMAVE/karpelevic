Topic VIII · Manuscript pages 83–86

Returning to stochastic eigenvalue regions

How does a radial boundary point new at order N satisfy the manuscript’s N-critical vertex-count conditions?

Topic orientationWhat this chapter proves and why it comes here

Topic VII proved Theorem 1.4's finite product equation conditionally for an N-critical planar map. Topic VIII now returns to row-stochastic matrices and verifies that the relevant radial boundary points satisfy that hypothesis.

Compactness, star-shapedness with respect to the origin, and the unit-circle classification make the radial function of each Karpelevič region precise. For N≥4, a radial boundary point that first appears at order N yields an N-critical multiplication map. Orders one, two, and three are reserved for the direct proof in Topic XIII.

Reading convention. The page first proves the stochastic eigenvalue-region facts that justify the radial function. It then restates the manuscript terms polygonal complexity, N-critical, and elliptic contraction with their literal formulas before the final proposition uses them. Complete manuscript proofs remain closed by default.

Prerequisites and sources

What this chapter uses

Every ingredient below has already been proved, is stated here with a precise source, or is established in full before the chapter uses it.

Imported from earlier topics

  • Topic I — polygonal complexity and N-criticality: defines νpoly(T) as the minimum invariant-polygon vertex count and calls T N-critical when νpoly(T)=N while νpoly(tT)>N for every t>1.
  • Topic III, Lemma A.5 — strict area monotonicity: justifies that two full-dimensional compact convex bodies of equal area cannot be properly nested.

Standard background, stated with sources

  • Finite-dimensional compactness: closed and bounded subsets of Euclidean space are compact; closed subsets and continuous images of compact sets are compact.
  • Real characteristic polynomials: nonreal roots occur in conjugate pairs, and eigenvalues are the zeros of det(λI−A). See Horn–Johnson, Matrix Analysis, Chapters 1 and 8.
  • Finite rotation or dense rotation: the powers of a unit complex number either form a finite root-of-unity orbit or are dense on the unit circle.
  • Supporting line at a boundary point: if zero lies on the boundary of a two-dimensional compact convex polygon, a nonzero real linear functional ℓ has ℓ≥0 on the polygon and kernel equal to a supporting line through zero.
  • Spectral radius: for a real-linear map A, spr(A) is the maximum modulus of its complex eigenvalues. The manuscript calls an elliptic map an elliptic contraction when spr(A) lies in (0,1).

Proved on this page

We prove compactness and star-shapedness of Θn, the exact equivalence between stochastic eigenpairs and invariant polytopes, the unit-circle classification, the interior-origin lemma, and the two literal conclusions νpoly(Tλ)=N and νpoly(tTλ)>N for every t>1 at a radial boundary point in ΘN∖ΘN−1.

Application of Topic VII

The conditional polygon theorem applies to radial boundary points

Topic VII proved the finite product theorem for an N-critical planar map when N≥4. The present topic supplies the missing link to stochastic matrices: a radial boundary point of ΘN that is not in ΘN−1 gives exactly the two vertex-count inequalities required by that theorem. No result from Topics IX–XIII is used here.

The object being studied

Row-stochastic matrices and their eigenvalue regions

A real row-stochastic matrix has nonnegative entries and every row sums to one. For a positive integer n, Θn is the set of all complex eigenvalues of all such n×n matrices. The matrices vary as well as the eigenvalue: Θn is the order-n Karpelevič region, assembled from an entire class of matrices. For λ∈ℂ, write Tλ:ℂ→ℂ for the real-linear map Tλ(z)=λz.

Why the subscript is matrix orderOpen the matrix-order interpretation

The order n counts stochastic states. Theorem II.4.2 proves that allowing n states is equivalent to allowing a non-singleton invariant polytope with at most n vertices. Thus increasing the matrix order increases the permitted number of vertices.

VIII.A

Stochastic eigenvalues and invariant polytopes

The first three statements establish the basic shape of Θn and prove that stochastic eigenpairs and finite invariant polytopes encode the same existence question.

  1. II.4.1
    Proposition II.4.1Classical result

    Compactness, symmetry under complex conjugation, and the disk bound

    Establishes the three global facts needed before the radial function is defined.

    Before reading the statementOpen the geometric idea and the role of the result

    IntuitionA stochastic row takes an average, so it cannot make the largest coordinate larger. Real characteristic polynomials reflect nonreal roots across the real axis, and a closed eigenpair equation prevents attainable roots from disappearing in a limit.

    Complete manuscript statement

    Proposition II.4.1 (Compactness, conjugation, and disk bound). For every nn, the set Θn\Theta_n is compact, invariant under complex conjugation, and contained in the closed unit disk.

    Complete proofOpen the manuscript proof and its guided explanation

    Proof. Let 𝒮n\mathcal S_n be the set of real row-stochastic n×nn\times n matrices. Every entry lies in [0,1][0,1], and the row-sum equations define a closed subset of [0,1]n2[0,1]^{n^2}; hence 𝒮n\mathcal S_n is compact. Write 𝔻¯={z∈ℂ:|z|≤1}.\overline{\mathbb D}=\{z\in\mathbb C:|z|\leq1\}. Every eigenvalue lies in 𝔻¯\overline{\mathbb D}: if Av=λvAv=\lambda v, choose an index with |vi|=∥v∥∞>0|v_i|=\|v\|_\infty>0; then |λ|∥v∥∞=|(Av)i|≤∑j=1naij|vj|≤∥v∥∞∑j=1naij=∥v∥∞.|\lambda|\|v\|_\infty =|(Av)_i| \leq\sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij}|v_j| \leq\|v\|_\infty\sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} =\|v\|_\infty. Consequently the eigenpair set ℰn={(A,λ)∈𝒮n×𝔻¯:det⁡(λI−A)=0}\mathcal E_n= \{(A,\lambda)\in\mathcal S_n\times\overline{\mathbb D}: \det(\lambda I-A)=0\} is closed in a compact space and is therefore compact. Its projection to the λ\lambda-coordinate is exactly Θn\Theta_n, so Θn\Theta_n is compact. Real characteristic polynomials give conjugation symmetry. ◻

    Guided proof
    1. 1
      Compact matrix space
      The row-sum equations and nonnegativity inequalities define a closed set. Every entry lies between zero and one, so the set of stochastic matrices is bounded and therefore compact.
    2. 2
      The maximum-coordinate estimate
      For an eigenvector choose an index whose coordinate has maximum modulus. The corresponding stochastic average has modulus at most that same maximum, giving |λ|≤1.

      Check. The chosen maximum is positive because an eigenvector is nonzero.

    3. 3
      A closed eigenpair set
      Inside the compact product of the stochastic matrices and the closed unit disk, the equation det(λI−A)=0 is a closed condition because the determinant is continuous.
    4. 4
      Project to the eigenvalue
      The projection of that compact eigenpair set to its second coordinate is exactly Θₙ, so Θₙ is compact.
    5. 5
      Reflect the root
      A real matrix has a characteristic polynomial with real coefficients; conjugating the equation preserves zero.
    Status and referencesSource classification and supporting references

    Classical result. The disk and symmetry statements occur in the classical theorem on stochastic eigenvalue regions; the compactness proof uses standard finite-dimensional topology.

    • W. Rudin, Principles of Mathematical Analysis, 3rd ed., McGraw–Hill (1976), Chapter 2, “Basic Topology.”

  2. II.4.2
    Theorem II.4.2Previously known

    Invariant-polytope criterion

    Proves the exact equivalence between a stochastic eigenpair and a finite invariant polytope.

    Definitions and notation used here2 entries to consult as needed
    Convex polytope
    The convex hull of finitely many points. In the plane this may be a segment; when it has nonempty interior it is a two-dimensional polygon.
    Open an example
    Inside a triangle, three barycentric weights describe a point. The weights need not be unique if redundant points are included; existence is enough here.
    Block-diagonal enlargement
    If an m×m stochastic matrix has the required eigenpair and m<n, replace it by A⊕Iₙ₋ₘ and append n−m zero coordinates to the eigenvector.
    Before reading the statementOpen the geometric idea and the role of the result

    IntuitionThe coordinates of a complex eigenvector are points in the plane. Each stochastic row says that the transformed coordinate λvᵢ is a convex average of all original coordinates. Conversely, convex-combination coefficients for the transformed vertices are already the rows of a stochastic matrix.

    Complete manuscript statement

    Theorem II.4.2 (Invariant-polytope criterion). Let n≥2n\geq2. For λ∈ℂ\lambda\in\mathbb C, the following are equivalent.

    1. λ∈Θn\lambda\in\Theta_n.

    2. There is a non-singleton convex polytope P⊆ℂ≃ℝ2P\subseteq\mathbb C\simeq\mathbb R^2 with at most nn vertices such that λP⊆P.\begin{equation} \label{karp:eq:invariant-polygon} \lambda P\subseteq P. \end{equation}(II.4.1)

    Complete proofOpen the manuscript proof and its guided explanation

    Proof. If Av=λvAv=\lambda v, put P=conv⁡{v1,…,vn}P=\mathop{\mathrm{conv}}\{v_1,\ldots,v_n\}. Every coordinate satisfies λvi=∑jaijvj∈P,\lambda v_i=\sum_j a_{ij}v_j\in P, so equation (II.4.1) holds. If the hull is a singleton, all coordinates of the nonzero eigenvector are equal to the same nonzero number, so λ=1\lambda=1; in that case any fixed segment may be used.

    Conversely, let x1,…,xmx_1,\ldots,x_m be the vertices of an invariant polytope, m≤nm\leq n. Choose barycentric coordinates λxi=∑j=1maijxj,aij≥0,∑jaij=1.\lambda x_i=\sum_{j=1}^m a_{ij}x_j, \qquad a_{ij}\geq0, \qquad \sum_j a_{ij}=1. Then A=(aij)A=(a_{ij}) is row-stochastic and Ax=λxAx=\lambda x. If m<nm<n, set A∼=A⊕In−m,x∼=(x1,…,xm,0,…,0)T.\widetilde A=A\oplus I_{n-m}, \qquad \widetilde x=(x_1,\ldots,x_m,0,\ldots,0)^T. Then A∼\widetilde A is row-stochastic and A∼x∼=λx∼\widetilde A\widetilde x=\lambda\widetilde x. ◻

    Guided proof
    1. 1
      Take the convex hull of the eigenvector coordinates
      Given Av=λv, place the coordinates v₁,…,vₙ in the complex plane and take their convex hull P.
    2. 2
      Every transformed coordinate stays inside
      The i-th eigenvector equation is a convex combination because row i is nonnegative and sums to one. Linear maps commute with convex hulls, so λP⊆P.
    3. 3
      Handle the singleton hull
      If all coordinates coincide, the nonzero eigenvector is constant and stochasticity forces λ=1. A fixed segment of positive length supplies the required non-singleton polytope.
    4. 4
      Use convex-combination coefficients as stochastic rows
      For each vertex xᵢ, choose convex-combination coefficients for λxᵢ in the polytope and put them in row i. The resulting matrix is row-stochastic and satisfies Ax=λx.
    5. 5
      Increase the matrix order by a direct sum
      If the polytope has m<n vertices, set Ã=A⊕Iₙ₋ₘ and x̃=(x₁,…,xₘ,0,…,0)ᵀ. Then Ãx̃=λx̃.

      Check. The same construction shows Θₘ⊆Θₙ whenever m≤n.

    ConclusionCounting stochastic states and counting vertices of an invariant polytope are the same existence problem; in particular, Θₘ⊆Θₙ for m≤n.

    Status and referencesSource classification and supporting references

    Previously known. Dmitriev and Dynkin use the invariant-polygon formulation; Swift supplies the English translation. The manuscript gives both directions explicitly.

    • J. Swift, The Location of Characteristic Roots of Stochastic Matrices, M.Sc. thesis, McGill University (1972), including an English translation of Dmitriev–Dynkin.
  3. II.4.3
    Corollary II.4.3Previously known

    Star-shapedness with respect to the origin

    Shows that every attainable point brings the entire segment from the origin with it.

    Definitions and notation used here2 entries to consult as needed
    Star-shapedness with respect to the origin
    If λ belongs to the region, then so does every tλ for 0≤t≤1.
    Strict area monotonicity
    If one planar convex body with interior is properly contained in another, its area is strictly smaller. See Topic III, Lemma A.5.
    Before reading the statementOpen the geometric idea and the role of the result

    IntuitionOnce an invariant polytope contains the origin, shrinking its transformed copy cannot leave it. The unit-modulus case first shows that the multiplier rotates a finite vertex orbit.

    Complete manuscript statement

    Corollary II.4.3 (Star-shapedness with respect to the origin). Let n≥2n\geq2. If λ∈Θn\lambda\in\Theta_n, then tλ∈Θnt\lambda\in\Theta_n for every 0≤t≤10\leq t\leq1.

    Complete proofOpen the manuscript proof and its guided explanation

    Proof. If |λ|<1|\lambda|<1, take an invariant polytope PP. Iteration gives λkP⊆P\lambda^kP\subseteq P and λkx→0\lambda^kx\to0, hence 0∈P0\in P; convexity then gives tλP⊆Pt\lambda P\subseteq P.

    Suppose |λ|=1|\lambda|=1, and take an invariant polytope PP with at most nn vertices. If PP has nonempty interior, equality of areas in λP⊆P\lambda P\subseteq P gives λP=P\lambda P=P by lem:strict-area-monotonicity. Thus multiplication by λ\lambda permutes the vertices, and the orbit of a nonzero vertex gives λk=1\lambda^k=1 for some k≤nk\leq n. If PP is a segment, equality of lengths again gives λP=P\lambda P=P; preservation of its line direction forces λ=±1\lambda=\pm1, whose orders are at most nn. Hence in all cases λ\lambda is a root of unity of order k≤nk\leq n.

    If λ=1\lambda=1, the segment [0,1][0,1] satisfies t[0,1]⊆[0,1]t[0,1]\subseteq[0,1]. Otherwise the regular orbit polygon Q=conv⁡{1,λ,…,λk−1}Q=\mathop{\mathrm{conv}}\{1,\lambda,\ldots,\lambda^{k-1}\} contains 00 because 1+λ+⋯+λk−1=01+\lambda+\cdots+\lambda^{k-1}=0, so 00 is the equal-weight average of its vertices. It satisfies λQ=Q\lambda Q=Q, and therefore tλQ=tQ⊆Qt\lambda Q=tQ\subseteq Q. The invariant-polytope criterion completes the proof. ◻

    Guided proof
    1. 1
      Iteration puts zero in the polytope
      When |λ|<1, the iterates λᵏx stay in the closed polytope and converge to zero.
    2. 2
      Convexity gives every smaller scalar multiple
      From 0∈P, convexity gives tP⊆P. Therefore (tλ)P=t(λP)⊆tP⊆P.
    3. 3
      Unit modulus forces equality
      If P has nonempty interior and |λ|=1, then λP and P have the same positive area. Inclusion plus strict area monotonicity forces λP=P.
    4. 4
      A finite vertex orbit
      Multiplication by λ permutes at most n vertices. A nonzero vertex returns after k≤n steps, so λᵏ=1.
    5. 5
      Take the convex hull of the root orbit
      Because 1+λ+⋯+λᵏ⁻¹=0, zero is the equal-weight average of the orbit points. Their convex hull is fixed by λ and contains every smaller scalar multiple.
    6. 6
      Handle the one-dimensional case
      A unit-modulus image segment has the same length as the original segment, so inclusion is equality. Its supporting real line is invariant, forcing λ=±1; the nontrivial case λ=−1 uses [−1,1].
    Status and referencesSource classification and supporting references

    Previously known. The radial/star-shaped structure is part of the established stochastic eigenvalue theory; the page proves it through invariant polygons.

    • N. Dmitriev and E. Dynkin, “On characteristic roots of stochastic matrices,” Izv. Akad. Nauk SSSR Ser. Mat. 10(2) (1946), 167–184.
    • J. Swift, The Location of Characteristic Roots of Stochastic Matrices, M.Sc. thesis, McGill University (1972), including an English translation of Dmitriev–Dynkin.

The radial function

Definition of the radial function

Let n≥2 and θ∈ℝ. The identity matrix shows that 1∈Θn, so Corollary II.4.3 gives 0∈Θn. We may therefore define

Rn(θ)=max{ρ∈[0,1] : ρeiθ∈Θn}.

Proposition II.4.1 makes the set of admissible radii compact, hence the maximum is attained. Star-shapedness from Corollary II.4.3 has a different role: it identifies the whole ray intersection as

Θn∩{ρeiθ:ρ≥0}={ρeiθ:0≤ρ≤Rn(θ)}.

VIII.B

The unit circle and the interior origin

The next two results classify the only unit-modulus points and show that a genuinely rotating strict contraction forces the origin into the interior of every non-singleton invariant polytope.

  1. II.4.4
    Proposition II.4.4Classical result

    Points of Θₙ on the unit circle

    Identifies exactly which attainable eigenvalues can have modulus one.

    Definitions and notation used here2 entries to consult as needed
    Root of unity of order k
    A complex number λ with λᵏ=1, where the least positive such k is its order.
    Cyclic permutation matrix
    A stochastic matrix that moves each basis state to the next and the last back to the first. Its eigenvalues are the corresponding roots of unity.
    Before reading the statementOpen the geometric idea and the role of the result

    IntuitionA unit-modulus rotation cannot fit a positive-area polygon strictly inside itself. It must permute a finite vertex set, and a finite rotation orbit is precisely a root of unity.

    Complete manuscript statement

    Proposition II.4.4 (Unit-circle points). Let n≥2n\geq2. If λ∈Θn\lambda\in\Theta_n and |λ|=1|\lambda|=1, then λ\lambda is a root of unity of order at most nn. Conversely every such root belongs to Θn\Theta_n.

    Complete proofOpen the manuscript proof and its guided explanation

    Proof. Take an invariant polytope PP with at most nn vertices. If PP has nonempty interior, then λP⊆P\lambda P\subseteq P and equality of areas forces λP=P\lambda P=P by lem:strict-area-monotonicity. Multiplication by λ\lambda permutes the vertices, so a nonzero vertex has an orbit of length at most nn and λk=1\lambda^k=1 for some k≤nk\leq n. If PP is a segment, then λP\lambda P and PP have the same positive length; inclusion therefore gives λP=P\lambda P=P. Thus multiplication by λ\lambda preserves the direction of the affine line containing PP, so λ=±1\lambda=\pm1.

    Conversely, if λ\lambda has order k≤nk\leq n, the permutation matrix of a kk-cycle is row-stochastic and has λ\lambda as an eigenvalue. If k<nk<n, take its direct sum with In−kI_{n-k}. ◻

    Guided proof
    1. 1
      Take an invariant polytope
      Theorem II.4.2 supplies one with at most n vertices.
    2. 2
      Area prevents strict inclusion
      In the two-dimensional case, λP and P have equal area. Inclusion therefore means equality.
    3. 3
      Permutation gives finite order
      A nonzero vertex returns within at most n vertices, so λᵏ=1 for some k≤n.
    4. 4
      The one-dimensional case
      If the polytope is a segment, equal length makes λP=P; a unit complex multiplier preserving its supporting real line is 1 or −1.
    5. 5
      Construct the converse
      The permutation matrix of a k-cycle realizes every k-th root of unity. If k<n, take its direct sum with Iₙ₋ₖ.
    Status and referencesSource classification and supporting references

    Classical result. This is the classical unit-circle clause of the Karpelevič theorem in Ito’s formulation, reproved here before any boundary arcs are used.

    • F. I. Karpelevič, “On the characteristic roots of matrices with nonnegative elements,” Izv. Akad. Nauk SSSR Ser. Mat. 15(4) (1951), 361–383.
    • H. Ito, “A new statement about the theorem determining the region of eigenvalues of stochastic matrices,” Linear Algebra and its Applications 267 (1997), 241–246.
    • R. A. Horn and C. R. Johnson, Matrix Analysis, 2nd ed., Cambridge University Press (2013), especially Chapter 8, “Positive and Nonnegative Matrices.”
  2. II.4.5
    Lemma II.4.5

    The origin is interior for a nonreal strict contraction

    Ensures that every invariant polytope relevant to an open upper-half-plane ray is two-dimensional and contains zero in its interior.

    Definitions and notation used here2 entries to consult as needed
    Supporting linear functional for P at 0
    A nonzero real-linear map ℓ such that P⊆{x:ℓ(x)≥0} and ℓ(0)=0. Its kernel is a supporting line of P at zero.
    Finite order or dense powers
    The powers of a unit complex number either form a finite root-of-unity orbit or are dense on the unit circle.
    Before reading the statementOpen the geometric idea and the role of the result

    IntuitionEvery orbit converges to zero. If zero lay on the boundary, one supporting half-plane would have to contain every rotated direction of a nonzero point, which is impossible for a genuinely nonreal rotation.

    Complete manuscript statement

    Lemma II.4.5 (Interior origin for a nonreal contraction). Let PP be a non-singleton compact convex polytope and let λ=ρeiθ\lambda=\rho e^{i\theta} with 0<ρ<10<\rho<1 and θ≢0,π(mod⁡2π)\theta\not\equiv0,\pi\pmod{2\pi}. If λP⊆P\lambda P\subseteq P, then PP has nonempty interior and 0∈int⁡P0\in\operatorname{int}P.

    Complete proofOpen the manuscript proof and its guided explanation

    Proof. Iteration gives 0∈P0\in P. If PP were a segment, its affine line would pass through 00 and would have to contain the segment of positive length λP\lambda P; hence multiplication by eiθe^{i\theta} would preserve a real line, forcing θ≡0\theta\equiv0 or π\pi, a contradiction. Thus PP has nonempty interior; equivalently, it is two-dimensional.

    Suppose 0∈∂P0\in\partial P. The supporting-line theorem gives a nonzero real linear functional ℓ\ell such that ℓ≥0\ell\geq0 on PP and ℓ(0)=0\ell(0)=0. Because PP has nonempty interior, it is not contained in ker⁡ℓ\ker\ell; hence we may choose z∈Pz\in P with ℓ(z)>0\ell(z)>0. For every k≥0k\geq0, invariance gives λkz=ρkeikθz∈P,0≤ℓ(λkz)=ρkℓ(eikθz).\lambda^kz=\rho^ke^{ik\theta}z\in P, \qquad 0\leq\ell(\lambda^kz) =\rho^k\ell(e^{ik\theta}z). Because ρk>0\rho^k>0, it follows that ℓ(eikθz)≥0\ell(e^{ik\theta}z)\geq0 for every k≥0k\geq0. If eiθe^{i\theta} has finite order mm, then m≥3m\geq3 and ∑k=0m−1eikθz=0\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}e^{ik\theta}z=0; summing the displayed nonnegative quantities forces them all to vanish, contrary to ℓ(z)>0\ell(z)>0. If eiθe^{i\theta} has infinite order, its powers are dense in the unit circle, so some eikθze^{ik\theta}z lies in the open half-plane where ℓ<0\ell<0, again a contradiction. Hence 00 is interior. ◻

    Guided proof
    1. 1
      Iteration reaches zero
      The points λᵏz stay in P and converge to zero, so closedness places zero in P.
    2. 2
      A segment of positive length is impossible
      Once zero lies in a segment of positive length, its affine hull is a real line through zero. A nonreal rotation cannot preserve that line.
    3. 3
      Choose a supporting linear functional for P at 0
      If zero were on the boundary, a supporting-line theorem would give a nonzero real-linear functional ℓ with ℓ≥0 on P and ℓ(0)=0. Since P has interior, choose z∈P with ℓ(z)>0.
    4. 4
      Retain the positive radial factor
      Invariance gives ρᵏeⁱᵏθz∈P, hence ρᵏℓ(eⁱᵏθz)≥0. Since ρᵏ>0, every ℓ(eⁱᵏθz) is nonnegative.
    5. 5
      Finite rotation contradiction
      The orbit vectors sum to zero. Their nonnegative ℓ-values also sum to zero, forcing ℓ(z)=0, contradiction.
    6. 6
      Infinite rotation contradiction
      Density puts some rotated point in the open half-plane where ℓ<0.
    ReferencesSources for the statement and its background

    This result is imported and reproved from Topic I, Lemma 2.5. Its supporting-line input is standard convex geometry; no literature-priority classification is asserted.

    • R. Schneider, Convex Bodies: The Brunn–Minkowski Theory, expanded ed., Cambridge University Press (2014): Chapter 1, §§1.1, 1.3, 1.6, 1.7, and Chapter 2, §§2.1, 2.4.

VIII.C

The manuscript’s vertex-count terminology

Definition II.4.6 restates the Topic I terminology here, before the stochastic boundary hypothesis uses it. Its two inequalities are always displayed alongside the term N-critical.

  1. II.4.6
    Definition II.4.6

    Polygonal complexity and the N-critical condition

    Restates the manuscript’s intrinsic terminology, with every symbol and both defining inequalities made literal.

    Definitions and notation used here5 entries to consult as needed
    Extreme-point set Ext(P)
    For a compact convex set P, Ext(P) consists of the points that do not lie in the open segment joining two distinct points of P. When P is a polytope, these extreme points are precisely its vertices.
    Polygonal complexity
    The value νpoly(A) is the minimum number of vertices of a compact convex polygon P with nonempty interior and AP⊆P; the value is ∞ if no such polygon exists.
    Spectral radius
    The value spr(A) is the maximum modulus of the complex eigenvalues of the real-linear map A.
    Elliptic contraction — manuscript terminology
    An elliptic real-linear map whose spectral radius lies in (0,1). For multiplication by λ, the Euclidean operator norm and spr(Tλ) both equal |λ|.
    N-critical — manuscript terminology
    The two conditions νpoly(T)=N and νpoly(tT)>N for every t>1.
    Before reading the statementOpen the geometric idea and the role of the result

    IntuitionThe first equality records the exact minimum vertex count for T. The second says that multiplying T by any scalar t>1 makes every invariant polygon with at most N vertices impossible.

    Complete manuscript statement

    Definition II.4.6 (Polygonal complexity and NN-critical maps). For a compact convex set PP, let Ext⁡(P)\mathop{\mathrm{Ext}}(P) denote its set of extreme points. For a real-linear map A:ℝ2→ℝ2A:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^2, let νpoly(A)=min⁡{|Ext(P)|:P is a compact convex polygon with nonempty interior,AP⊆P}.\nu_{\mathrm{poly}}(A) =\min\left\{\left|\mathop{\mathrm{Ext}}(P)\right|: \begin{array}{c} P\text{ is a compact convex polygon with nonempty interior},\\ AP\subseteq P \end{array} \right\}. Invariant-polytope equivalence

Eigenvalue membership is a vertex-count bound

Let n≥2 and let λ be nonreal with 0<|λ|<1. Theorem II.4.2 and Lemma II.4.5 give the exact equivalence

λ∈Θn ⇔ νpoly(Tλ)≤n.

Indeed, membership supplies a non-singleton invariant polytope with at most n vertices, and Lemma II.4.5 makes it two-dimensional, so it is counted by νpoly. Conversely, any polygon counted by νpoly satisfies the invariant-polytope criterion. This is the implication used below in both directions.

The extremal condition

A radial boundary point new at order N

Fix N≥4 and 0<θ<π, and suppose

λ=RN(θ)eiθ∈ΘN∖ΘN−1, 0<|λ|<1. (II.4.3)

The condition 0<|λ|<1 excludes the origin and the already classified unit circle. The membership λ∈ΘN∖ΘN−1 says that λ occurs as an eigenvalue at order N but not at order N−1.

Region nesting gives RN−1(θ)≤RN(θ). The endpoint for ΘN−1 is attained; equality would therefore put λ in ΘN−1, contrary to the displayed hypothesis. Hence

RN−1(θ)<RN(θ).

The exclusion from ΘN−1 rules out every invariant polygon with at most N−1 vertices. Radial maximality rules out every invariant polygon with at most N vertices fortTλ when t>1. Proposition II.4.7 writes these vertex-count conclusions as νpoly(Tλ)=N and νpoly(tTλ)>N.
