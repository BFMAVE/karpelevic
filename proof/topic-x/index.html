Topic X · Manuscript pages 86–91

The radial upper bound and its equality case

For an upper-half-plane radial boundary point that first appears at order N, why is its modulus no greater than the Topic IX radius on the corresponding Farey interval?

Topic orientationWhat this chapter proves and why it comes here

For an upper-half-plane radial boundary point that first appears at order N≥4, Topics VII and VIII supply a finite product equation, a phase equation for chosen arguments of its factors, and bounds placing those arguments in one interval. Lower-half-plane points follow by complex conjugation.

Topic X turns those identities into a Jensen comparison with the candidate radius constructed in Topic IX and characterizes equality under the finite-product hypotheses. Topic XI, now published, independently realizes the common-parameter case; Topic XII handles inherited lower-order boundary points through nesting.

Imported from earlier topics

  • Topic VII — consecutive Farey fractions and a finite product equation: supplies, for N≥4, the finite product equation with possibly different real parameters, the exact phase equation for chosen arguments of its factors, and bounds placing those arguments in one interval.
  • Topic VIII — criticality of a radial boundary point new at order N: shows that such a boundary point determines the N-critical planar contraction required by Topic VII.
  • Topic IX — the unique modulus from the Ito equation: provides the unique modulus at each prescribed argument and the real equation used for comparison.

Standard background, stated with sources

  • Jensen’s inequality: for a strictly convex function, the value at an average is at most the average of the values, with equality only when all inputs coincide.
  • Arguments in the open upper half-plane: every nonzero complex number in the open upper half-plane has a unique argument in (0,π).

Proved on this page

We apply Part I's finite product equation to the stochastic boundary point, relate the two possible orientations by complex conjugation, and apply Jensen's inequality to an elementary strictly convex function obtained from the triangle sine ratios. The result compares the point's modulus with Topic IX's candidate radius.

Scope

Hypotheses and the comparison proved here

Fix N≥4 and 0<θ<π. Let ΘN be the union of the spectra of all real row-stochastic N×N matrices and let RN(θ)=max{r≥0: reiθ∈ΘN}. Fix λ=RN(θ)eiθ∈ΘN∖ΘN−1, with 0<|λ|<1. Thus λ is a radial boundary point in the open upper half-plane that first appears at order N. The lower-half-plane case follows by complex conjugation.

Put x=θ/(2π). Let FNdenote the Farey sequence of order N. Topic VII chooses μ∈{λ,λ̄}. Set ρ:=|μ|=|λ|, and let ϑ∈(0,2π) be the unique number satisfying μ=ρeiϑ. Put y=ϑ/(2π). Topic VII provides consecutive fractions p/q<y<r/s in FNwith q<s. Define d=⌊N/q⌋, e=s−dq, A=qϑ−2πp, and B=(2πr−sϑ)/d.

Theorem II.5.1 lists the resulting product equation, phase equation, and bounds on the arguments of μᑫ−βⱼ. Lemma II.5.2 transfers the comparison back to λ when μ=λ̄. Retaining ϑ as this real number, rather than reducing the phase equation modulo 2π, makes the average in Theorem II.6.1 exact.

Define ρ* using Topic IX's radius on the original upper-half-plane Farey interval. If μ=λ, set ρ*=ρp/q,r/s(N)(x). If μ=λ̄, reflection gives (s−r)/s<x<(q−p)/q, and set ρ*=ρ(s−r)/s,(q−p)/q(N)(x). In each case the two subscripts are the endpoints in left-to-right order in FN+, and Topic IX proves that this is the unique solution in (0,1) of its scalar equation.

This page proves ρ≤ρ* and characterizes equality under the finite-product and phase hypotheses. The argument itself does not construct a stochastic matrix at ρ*. Topic XI, now published, supplies that independent construction and proves attainment in ΘN; Topic XII uses nesting to handle boundary points inherited from lower orders.

Convexity

The strictly convex function and the factor arguments

For whichever choice μ=λ or μ=λ̄ Topic VII supplies, retain the quantities ρ, ϑ, y, p/q, r/s, d, e, A, and B defined in the Scope. Farey adjacency gives rq−ps=1.

p/q<y<r/s, rq−ps=1, q<s. (II.6.1)

A=qϑ−2πp, B=(2πr−sϑ)/d. (II.6.2)

A>0, B>0, A+B<π. (II.6.3)

Set z=μᑫ=ρᑫeiA. Let M∈(0,π) be the unique angle such that μᑫ−1=|μᑫ−1|eiM; then A<M<π. For 0≤β<1, let u(β)∈(0,π) be the unique angle satisfying μᑫ−β=|μᑫ−β|eiu(β). The imported argument bounds give u(β)∈[A,M).

Consider the triangle with vertices β, 1, and z. Its angles at β, 1, and z are respectively u(β), π−M, and M−u(β). The sine rule gives

|z−β|/sin M=(1−β)/sin(M−u(β)).

Define F:[A,M)→ℝ by F(u)=log(sin M)−log(sin(M−u)). The displayed sine-rule identity is exactly the following equation.

F(u(β))=log(|μᑫ−β|/(1−β)). (II.6.4)

The second derivative is

F″(u)=csc²(M−u)>0. (II.6.5)

Therefore F is strictly convex on [A,M). Moreover, du/dβ=ρᑫ sin A/|μᑫ−β|²>0. The phase equation eϑ+Σⱼ₌₁ᵈuⱼ=2π(r−dp) fixes the mean of the arguments at A+B. Taking absolute values and logarithms in the assumed product equation fixes Σⱼ₌₁ᵈF(uⱼ). Jensen's inequality compares this list with the list in which all arguments equal their common mean.

Since A<A+B<M<π, the quantities sin A, sin B, sin(A+B), sin M, sin(M−A), and sin(M−A−B) are positive. These signs justify the logarithms, divisions, and multiplications used below and preserve the direction of the inequality.

At β=0, one has u(0)=A, so the sine-rule identity gives ρᑫ=sin M/sin(M−A). (II.6.6)

Jensen’s inequality and the radial upper bound

    Radial inequality under the finite-product and phase hypotheses

    Uses Jensen’s inequality for the strictly convex function F(u)=log(sin M)−log(sin(M−u)) to prove ρ≤ρ*, with equality exactly when all βⱼ agree.

      Taking absolute values and logarithms in the assumed product equation, and using αⱼ=1−βⱼ>0, gives Σⱼ₌₁ᵈF(uⱼ)=(dq−s)logρ.
    5. 5
      Check every sign used later
      From A<A+B<M<π, the sines of A, B, A+B, M, M−A, and M−A−B are positive.
    6. 6
      Apply Jensen’s inequality
      Every uⱼ and their mean A+B lie in [A,M), where F is strictly convex. Hence dF(A+B)≤(dq−s)logρ, with equality exactly when all uⱼ coincide.
    7. 7
      Identify the equality case
      The explicit derivative du/dβ=ρᑫ sin A/|μᑫ−β|²>0 shows that all uⱼ coincide exactly when all βⱼ coincide.
    8. 8
      Exponentiate the inequality
      Substituting F and exponentiating gives the sine-ratio inequality. The preceding positivity check justifies every division and preserves the inequality direction.
    9. 9
      Use the trigonometric reduction
      Equation (II.6.10), together with ρᑫ=sin M/sin(M−A), converts the sine-ratio inequality into the scalar radial inequality (II.6.7).
    10. 10
      Compare with Topic IX’s unique solution
      The scalar left side is strictly increasing in ρ and equals sin(A+B) once, at the indexed Topic IX radius ρ*. Therefore (II.6.7) is equivalent to ρ≤ρ*.

    ConclusionFor every list satisfying the finite product equation, the phase equation, and the bounds uⱼ∈[A,M), one has ρ≤ρ*. Equality holds in this radial inequality—equivalently, ρ=ρ*—if and only if β₁=···=βd. Topic XI proves that this common-parameter case is attained by a stochastic matrix.
