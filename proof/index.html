Topic I · 2 definitions · 7 results

Critical maps and invariant polygons

How can the polygon problem be stated without tying it to a particular matrix or coordinate system?

Reading mode
Topic orientationWhy these definitions and results come first

We work on a two-dimensional real vector space and study how a linear transformation acts on compact convex polygons. The first task is to define the transformation, the polygons it preserves, and the least number of vertices such a polygon can have.

Only after that vocabulary is fixed do we define radial criticality. The rest of the chapter proves that these objects behave naturally under changes of coordinates, fixes the orientation conventions, and shows that every nontrivial invariant polygon surrounds the origin.

Guided foundationsDefinitions and examples before the formal text

1 · Definitions

The objects, one at a time

Nothing in the first formal definition is left implicit. The list below fixes the ambient space, the transformation, the polygon, the invariance condition, and the two numerical quantities that describe the transformation.

Real plane
A vector space V of dimension two over the real numbers. It has vector addition and multiplication by real scalars, but no preferred axes, lengths, angles, or complex coordinate.
Real-linear map
A function A : V → V satisfying A(x + y) = Ax + Ay and A(cx) = cAx for all vectors x, y and real scalars c. Once a basis is chosen, this is an ordinary real 2 × 2 matrix.
Compact nondegenerate convex polygon
The convex hull of finitely many points, bounded and closed, with nonempty two-dimensional interior. “Nondegenerate” rules out a single point or a line segment. Its extreme points form Ext(P), the set of extreme points of P.
Why must the polygon be compact?

In the usual finite-dimensional definition, a convex polygon is the convex hull of finitely many points and is therefore automatically closed, bounded, and compact. The adjective is written explicitly because the proof uses those properties: extrema are attained, supporting faces exist, iterated points retain their limit, area is finite, and Hausdorff limits remain available.

Allowing arbitrary unbounded polyhedral sets would define a different and largely degenerate problem. For example, the whole plane satisfies AV ⊆ V for every linear map and has no extreme points, so a vertex-count minimum would immediately collapse to zero.

Invariant polygon
A polygon P is invariant under A when AP ⊆ P: applying A to every point of P produces a set contained in P. Equality is not required.
Boundary, affine hull, and relative interior
For every set K, the boundary is ∂K = K \ int(K), where the overline denotes closure. Since every compact convex set used here is closed, this reduces to K \ int(K). The affine hull aff(S) is the smallest affine subspace containing S. The relative interior relint(S) is the interior measured inside aff(S); for a side, it is the open segment without its endpoints.
Relative to what—and is there an absolute interior?

Unqualified “interior” means interior in the ambient space. A line segment in the plane has empty ambient interior because no two-dimensional open disk fits inside it. Its affine hull is its supporting line, and relative to that line the segment does have an interior: the open segment obtained by removing both endpoints.

“Absolute interior” is not standard terminology; the usual contrast is between ambient interior and relative interior.

Example 1 · A segment in the plane

Let L = {(t,0) | 0 ≤ t ≤ 1}. As a subset of ℝ², L has empty interior: no open disk fits inside it. But aff(L) is the x-axis, and relint(L) = {(t,0) | 0 < t < 1}. Thus the relative interior is the segment without its two endpoints.

For the filled triangle Δ = conv({(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)}) in ℝ², aff(Δ) = ℝ², so relint(Δ) = int(Δ): the points satisfying x > 0, y > 0, and x + y < 1. For its bottom side E = [(0,0),(1,0)], however, int(E) is empty in ℝ² while relint(E) is the open bottom edge.

Supporting line, exposed face, and normal cone
A supporting line touches a convex set while the set remains in one closed half-plane. The touched points form an exposed face. At a vertex, the supporting nonzero linear functionals in the dual plane V* form its normal cone. After choosing an auxiliary inner product, these covectors may be drawn as familiar normal arrows.
Positive orientation and cyclic order
The positive boundary direction keeps the polygon’s interior on the left. A cyclic order records which vertices or sides follow which around that boundary, without choosing a permanent starting point.
Real-linear conjugacy
The maps T and A T A−1 describe the same dynamics after the invertible coordinate change A. An intrinsic quantity should have the same value for both.
Polygonal complexity
The number νpoly(A) is the smallest possible number of extreme points among all nondegenerate compact convex polygons invariant under A. It is ∞ if no such polygon exists.
Elliptic map
An invertible real-linear map T is called elliptic when tr(T)2 < 4 det(T). This says that the discriminant of its characteristic polynomial is negative, so its two eigenvalues are a nonreal conjugate pair.
Spectral radius
The spectral radius ρ(T) is the largest absolute value of an eigenvalue of T. For an elliptic map the two eigenvalues have the same modulus, so ρ(T) = √det(T).
Elliptic contraction
An elliptic map whose spectral radius lies strictly between 0 and 1. In suitable coordinates it rotates the plane through a nonzero, non-half-turn angle and shrinks every distance by the common factor ρ(T).

The same setup in compact mathematical form

The displayed minimum below packages the first five definitions into one coordinate-free quantity. Read Ext(P) as “the extreme points of P.”

Let VV be a real plane. For every real-linear map A:V→VA:V\to V, define its polygonal complexity by νpoly(A)=min⁡{|Ext(P)|:P⊂V is a nondegenerate compact convex polygon and AP⊆P},\begin{equation} \label{eq:polygonal-complexity} \nu_{\mathrm{poly}}(A)=\min\Bigl\{\left|\mathop{\mathrm{Ext}}(P)\right|: P\subset V\text{ is a nondegenerate compact convex polygon and }AP\subseteq P\Bigr\}, \end{equation}(1.1) with the value ∞\infty when the class is empty. Definition 1.1

Radial polygonal criticality

Defines N-criticality by comparing the least invariant-polygon vertex count for T with the counts for every tT with t>1.

Complete definition

Definition 1.1 (Radial polygonal criticality). For N≥3N\geq3, an elliptic contraction TT is NN-critical if νpoly(T)=N,νpoly(tT)>Nfor every t>1.\begin{equation} \label{eq:N-critical} \nu_{\mathrm{poly}}(T)=N, \qquad \nu_{\mathrm{poly}}(tT)>N\quad\text{for every }t>1. \end{equation}(1.2) The second inequality allows the value ∞\infty.

Both conditions are invariant under real-linear conjugacy. They are therefore properties of the planar dynamics, not of a chosen Euclidean metric, complex coordinate, polygon, or vertex numbering.

We write Ext⁡(K)\mathop{\mathrm{Ext}}(K) for the set of extreme points of a compact convex set KK, ∂K\partial K for its boundary, relint⁡(F)\mathop{\mathrm{relint}}(F) for relative interior, and aff⁡(S)\mathop{\mathrm{aff}}(S) for affine hull.

Optional explanationReading the definition

Why this definition is neededPolygonal complexity is an optimisation problem over every invariant polygon, not a property of one convenient drawing. The equality νpoly(T)=N says that N is the least number of vertices of such a polygon. Radial criticality adds that, for every t>1, no polygon with at most N vertices is invariant under tT.

  1. 1
    The first condition

    The equality νpoly(T)=N says both that an invariant N-gon exists and that no invariant polygon with fewer than N extreme points exists.

  2. 2
    The radial condition

    For every t>1, the map tT has polygonal complexity greater than N. Multiplication by t scales every output of T by the positive factor t without changing its angular action. The value may be infinite because tT need not remain a contraction.

  3. 3
    Why the quantifier is strict

    Criticality is not merely the existence of an invariant N-gon. It says that every tT with t>1 requires more than N vertices, which is the extremal condition used later.

Keep in mindRead N-critical as “N is the least possible vertex count for T, and no polygon with at most N vertices works for any tT with t>1.”

Definition 1.2

Rules out repeated vertices and flat turns so that every side and cyclic order used later is unambiguous.

Definitions and figuresWhat do “maximal side,” “normal cone,” and “strict supporting line” mean?

1 · Extreme points only

Adjacent and collinear sides

Two sides are adjacent when they occur one after the other along the polygon boundary and share an endpoint. They are collinear when both lie on the same straight line. Thus, if m lies between a and b, the adjacent segments [a,m] and [m,b] are collinear: the boundary does not turn at m, so m is not an extreme point.

A maximal boundary segment is a straight segment contained in the boundary that cannot be extended at either end while remaining a straight boundary segment. In the left-hand picture below, [a,b] is maximal; neither of its two shorter pieces is. At an extreme point of the polygon, by contrast, the boundary changes direction.

The manuscript calls a polygon strict when its displayed vertex list contains every extreme point exactly once and no other points. Thus the list has no repeated vertices or points inserted along a side. This is custom manuscript terminology; it does not mean “strictly convex” in the standard convex-geometric sense.

2 · How a line can touch a corner

Normal cones and strict supporting lines

A supporting line through a vertex v leaves the entire polygon in one of its two closed half-planes. Its contact face is simply the set of polygon points lying on that line. The contact face can be a whole side, or it can be the single point {v}.

The intrinsic normal cone at v lives in the dual plane V*:

NP(v) = {ℓ ∈ V* : ℓ(x−v) ≤ 0 for every x ∈ P}.

Thus its elements are linear functionals, or covectors—not intrinsically vectors with a dot product. If we choose an auxiliary inner product, every covector ℓ can be represented by a normal arrow n; the wedge of arrows in the drawing is this convenient representation of the dual cone. At an extreme point the normal cone is two-dimensional and has nonempty interior. At a point inserted in the interior of a side, the normal cone is only one ray.

A nonzero covector on the boundary of the cone exposes an entire incident side. A covector in the interior produces a supporting line whose contact face is exactly {v}. Such a line is called a strict supporting line at v.

Both lines pictured below pass through the vertex v. The left one is not strict, because it also contains every point of an incident side; the right one is strict, because its contact face is the singleton {v}.

What the manuscript sentence is saying.Once the displayed vertex list contains exactly the extreme points, there are no flat turns. Consequently every displayed side is already the longest straight piece of boundary in that direction, and the normal cone at every vertex has an open set of covectors that expose that vertex alone.
Optional explanationReading the definition

Why this definition is neededA polygon can be displayed with repeated points or with extra points inserted along a side. Those choices must not alter the complexity. The manuscript calls a polygon strict when its displayed list contains each extreme point exactly once and no other points. This removes redundant entries before sides and cyclic order are used.

  1. 1
    Extreme points only

    The displayed cyclic list contains every point of Ext(P) exactly once and contains no other points. A point in the relative interior of a side is a convex combination of its endpoints and therefore is not a vertex. The manuscript’s custom word “strict” does not mean strictly convex in the standard sense.

  2. 2
    Maximal sides

    Adjacent sides are consecutive boundary segments sharing an endpoint. If they lie on the same line, their shared endpoint is not an extreme point and their union is one longer segment. A maximal side is this longest straight boundary segment: it cannot be extended at either end while remaining in the boundary.

  3. 3
    Strict support

    The contact face of a supporting line is the part of the polygon lying on that line. A supporting line is strict when its contact face is the single vertex rather than a complete edge. Intrinsically, the exposing object is a nonzero covector in the dual plane; after an auxiliary inner product identifies covectors with normal arrows, such a line is strict exactly when its arrow lies in the interior of the vertex’s normal cone.

Keep in mindFor a strict polygon in the manuscript’s sense, every displayed vertex is an extreme point and every displayed side is a maximal boundary segment.

  1. 2.1
    Proposition 2.1

    Adapted complex structures

    Identifies an elliptic real-linear map with multiplication by one of its two conjugate complex eigenvalues.

    First-use vocabularyDefinitions first used in this result
    Complex structure
    A real-linear map J:V→V satisfying J²=−I. If multiplication by i is defined to mean application of J, then the real plane V becomes a one-dimensional complex vector space.
    Orientation induced by J
    The orientation in which every ordered basis (v,Jv), with v≠0, is positive. Replacing J by −J reverses this orientation.
    Adapted inner product
    An inner product for which J is an isometry. In that geometry, T=ρ(cosθ I+sinθ J) is literally a rotation through θ followed by a contraction by ρ.
    Linear-algebra notationWhat is the adjoint in the adapted inner product?

    Once an inner product ⟨·,·⟩ has been chosen, the adjoint of a real-linear map A is the unique linear map A* satisfying

    ⟨Ax,y⟩ = ⟨x,A*y⟩   for every x,y.

    In an orthonormal basis, A* is represented by the transpose matrix. The word “orthonormal” matters: after a non-orthogonal change of coordinates, simply transposing the displayed matrix need not represent the adjoint for the original inner product.

    Proposition 2.1 constructs an inner product for which J+ is an isometry, meaning that it preserves the inner product and therefore lengths and angles. Because J+2=−I, its inverse is −J+. An isometry satisfies J+*=J+−1, so here J+*=−J+. This is the identity used to compute T*.

    Complete statement

    Proposition 2.1 (Adapted complex structures). Let TT be elliptic. Then det⁡(T)>0\det(T)>0. Put ρ=det⁡(T),cos⁡θ=tr⁡(T)2ρ,0<θ<π,\rho=\sqrt{\det(T)}, \qquad \cos\theta=\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(T)}{2\rho}, \qquad 0<\theta<\pi, and define J+=ρ−1T−cos⁡θIsin⁡θ,J−=−J+.\begin{equation} \label{eq:adapted-J} J_+=\frac{\rho^{-1}T-\cos\theta\,I}{\sin\theta}, \qquad J_-=-J_+. \end{equation}(2.1) Then J+2=J−2=−IJ_+^2=J_-^2=-I, and the two complex structures induce the two opposite orientations of VV. There is an inner product for which both are isometries and T=ρ(cos⁡θI+sin⁡θJ+)=ρ(cos⁡θI−sin⁡θJ−).\begin{equation} \label{eq:T-similarity-form} T=\rho(\cos\theta\,I+\sin\theta\,J_+) =\rho(\cos\theta\,I-\sin\theta\,J_-). \end{equation}(2.2) Thus TT is multiplication by ρeiθ\rho e^{i\theta} in the J+J_+ complex coordinate and by ρe−iθ\rho e^{-i\theta} in the J−J_- coordinate. In the adapted inner product, T*=ρ(cos⁡θI−sin⁡θJ+),T^*=\rho(\cos\theta\,I-\sin\theta\,J_+), and T*T^* is conjugate to TT by an orientation-reversing reflection.

Hence J+J_+ and J−J_- induce precisely the two orientations of VV.

    Optional explanationOpen intuition and proof walkthrough

    IntuitionThe real plane has no preferred complex coordinate. An elliptic map supplies one: after normalisation, its nonreal part squares to −I and therefore acts as multiplication by i.

    1. 1

      Normalise the eigenvalues

      Write the conjugate eigenvalues as ρe^{±iθ}, where ρ=√det(T) and cos θ=tr(T)/(2ρ). Ellipticity ensures 0<θ<π, so sin θ is nonzero.

    2. 2

      Construct the complex structure

      Set J+=(ρ⁻¹T−cos θ I)/sin θ. Cayley–Hamilton reduces the square of the numerator to −sin²θ I, hence J+²=−I.

    3. 3

      Choose the orientation

      The structures J+ and J−=−J+ induce opposite orientations and make T multiplication by ρe^{iθ} and ρe^{-iθ}, respectively.

    4. 4

      Choose an adapted metric

      Averaging any inner product with its pullback by J+ makes J+ orthogonal. In that metric, T is literally a rotation through θ followed by contraction by ρ.

    Keep in mindThe complex picture is intrinsic up to conjugation: it packages the real map as rotation plus contraction without selecting arbitrary Euclidean axes.

  2. 2.2
    Proposition 2.2

    Real-linear invariance of polygonal complexity

    Shows that changing real coordinates does not alter the least number of vertices needed for an invariant polygon.

    Complete statement

    Proposition 2.2 (Real-linear invariance of polygonal complexity). For every invertible real-linear map AA, νpoly(ATA−1)=νpoly(T).\nu_{\mathrm{poly}}(ATA^{-1})=\nu_{\mathrm{poly}}(T). Consequently NN-criticality is invariant under conjugacy, and νpoly(tT*)=νpoly(tT)\nu_{\mathrm{poly}}(tT^*)=\nu_{\mathrm{poly}}(tT) for every t>0t>0 in the adapted inner product.

    ProofOpen the proof of Proposition 2.2

    Proof. The correspondence P↦APP\mapsto AP preserves convexity, nondegeneracy, the number of extreme points, and inclusion. Moreover TP⊆P⇔(ATA−1)(AP)⊆AP.TP\subseteq P \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad (ATA^{-1})(AP)\subseteq AP. The first assertion follows. The second uses the reflection conjugacy in Proposition 2.1. ◻

    Detailed versionOpen the six-step proof

    Expanded proof. Let A be invertible and introduce the conjugate map and transported polygon

    T∼=ATA−1,P∼=AP.
    1. The candidate polygons correspond bijectively.

      The assignment P ↦ AP has inverse Q ↦ A−1Q. Because A is a linear homeomorphism, it preserves compactness and maps line segments to line segments. Hence it preserves convexity. It also maps open sets to open sets, so a polygon has nonempty interior exactly when its image does. Thus nondegenerate compact convex polygons correspond in both directions.

    2. The correspondence preserves extreme points.

      Suppose x is extreme in P and Ax = s y1 + (1−s)y2, where 0<s<1 and y1, y2∈AP. Write yj=Axj with xj∈P, and apply A−1. Then x=s x1+(1−s)x2. Extremality forces x1=x2=x. Thus Ax is extreme in AP. Applying the same argument to A−1 gives the reverse inclusion, so

      Ext(AP)=A(Ext(P)).

      In particular, P and AP have exactly the same number of vertices.

    3. Invariance is transported exactly.

      Applying an injective map preserves and reflects set inclusion. Therefore

      TP⊆P⟺T∼P∼=(ATA−1)(AP)=A(TP)⊆AP=P∼.

      The displayed implication can be read in either direction: applying A−1 recovers TP⊆P from the transported inclusion.

    4. The two minimisation problems are identical.

      We have a bijection between all polygons satisfying TP⊆P and all polygons satisfying the corresponding inclusion for ATA−1, and the bijection preserves the number of extreme points. The two sets of possible vertex counts are therefore equal. Their minima agree; if either set of candidate polygons is empty, both are empty and both complexities equal ∞. Hence

      νpoly(ATA−1)=νpoly(T).
    5. Radial criticality is preserved as well.

      For every scalar t>0,

      A(tT)A−1=t(ATA−1).

      The equality just proved therefore holds simultaneously for T and every radial comparison map tT. Consequently the conditions νpoly(T)=N and νpoly(tT)>N for all t>1 hold exactly when the corresponding conditions hold for ATA−1. This proves conjugacy invariance of N-criticality.

    6. The adjoint identity is the reflection case.

      Proposition 2.1 supplies an orientation-reversing reflection S in the adapted inner product such that STS−1=T*. Hence S(tT)S−1=tT*. Applying the first part with A=S gives νpoly(tT*)=νpoly(tT) for every t>0.

    This proves every assertion of Proposition 2.2. □

  3. 2.3
    Proposition 2.3

    Real-linear covariance of contact geometry

    Shows that an invertible coordinate change preserves vertices, sides, boundary intersections, and the side data in equation (2.3).

    First-use vocabularyDefinitions first used in this result
    A functional exposes a face
    A nonzero real-linear functional ℓ exposes the face F of a compact convex polygon P when F={x∈P:ℓ(x)=max{ℓ(y):y∈P}}. The illustrated menu immediately below shows the level-line construction and a whole-side example.
    Clipping
    Intersecting a polygon with a closed half-plane so that a boundary cap is removed. Applying A before or after this operation gives the same transformed polygon.
    Covariance
    A construction is covariant when a change of coordinates transports every object it produces—vertices, sides, faces, contacts, and labels—in a compatible way.
    The side data in equation (2.3)
    The set ℰ(P) consists of the oriented sides of P, and s(e) is the next side. The map χ assigns each vertex v to a side containing Tv. The set I consists of those assigned sides for which Tv lies in the relative interior, while h(e) is the ending vertex of e and σ(e)=χ(h(e)) is a cyclic shift of the side labels. Finally, A_ℰ sends each side e of P to the side Ae of AP.
    Topological vocabularyWhat does “homeomorphism” contribute here?

    A homeomorphism is a continuous bijection whose inverse is also continuous. It preserves topological features such as open and closed sets, interior, boundary, and connectedness, although it need not preserve lengths or angles.

    Every invertible real-linear map A on a finite-dimensional plane is a homeomorphism: both A and its linear inverse A−1 are continuous. The proof of Proposition 2.3 uses more than topology as well—linearity preserves convex combinations and line segments. The homeomorphism property preserves ambient interiors and boundaries; applying the same argument to the restriction of A between the relevant affine hulls preserves relative interiors.

    Local geometric vocabularyWhat does it mean for a functional to expose a face?

    A nonzero real-linear functional ℓ assigns a real number ℓ(x) to every point x. Its level sets are parallel straight lines. Moving those lines in the direction in which ℓ increases eventually reaches the last line that still meets the compact polygon P.

    Write c = max {ℓ(x) : x ∈ P}. Then P lies in the half-plane ℓ(x) ≤ c, while the final level line is ℓ(x) = c. The set

    F = {x ∈ P : ℓ(x) = c}

    is the face exposed by ℓ. It consists of every point where ℓ attains its maximum—not merely one arbitrarily chosen point of intersection. It may be a whole side, as below, or a single vertex. In the single-vertex case, the corresponding supporting line is strict.

    Complete statement and proof

    Proposition 2.3 (Real-linear covariance of contact geometry). Let A:V→VA:V\to V be invertible, put T∼=ATA−1,P∼=AP,\widetilde T=ATA^{-1}, \qquad \widetilde P=AP, and suppose that PP is a strict polygon. Then the following statements hold.

    Equation (2.3), unpackedHow does a coordinate change transport the side data?

    The coordinate change A sends the polygon P to AP. It therefore sends each side e of P to the corresponding side Ae of AP. The notation Aℰ names precisely this side-by-side relabelling:

    On PAℰ →On AP
    side e ∈ I, with its assigned image point in the relative interior→side Ae in the corresponding target set
    next side s(e)→next side after Ae
    cyclic shift σ(e)→corresponding cyclic shift of Ae

    The three identities say that these target objects are exactly the transported versions of the original ones. Equivalently: rename each side by applying A, then take the next side or apply the cyclic shift. The result is the same as applying the operation first and then renaming the resulting side by A.

    “Conjugacy” therefore means the same combinatorial rule after a change of labels. It does not mean that P and AP are literally the same polygon.

  4. 2.4
    Lemma 2.4

    Coordinate reversal, orientation, and endpoint assignment

    Records how orientation, the chosen complex eigenvalue, and half-open endpoint membership change under a reversing coordinate map.

    First-use vocabularyDefinitions first used in this result
    Assignment to right-half-open sides
    Every image vertex Tv is assigned to a side whose right-half-open segment (tail, head] contains Tv. Reflection changes this segment into one that includes the opposite endpoint, so endpoint assignments must be checked again.
    Convergence of moving polygonsWhat exactly is Hausdorff convergence?

    For a point x and a nonempty compact set L, write dist(x,L) for the smallest distance from x to a point of L. The Hausdorff distance between two nonempty compact sets K and L is

    dH(K,L) =max{ supx∈K dist(x,L), supy∈L dist(y,K) }.

    Thus dH(K,L)≤ε means two things at once: every point of K lies within ε of L, and every point of L lies within ε of K. A family Pτ converges to P0 in the Hausdorff sense when dH(Pτ,P0)→0 as τ→0.

    A moving polygon

    If corresponding vertices of two polygons are all within ε, then corresponding convex combinations are within ε. Hence moving every vertex continuously makes the polygon move continuously in Hausdorff distance.

    Why conjugation preserves it

    Complex conjugation C is an isometry: |Cx−Cy|=|x−y|. It therefore preserves every point-to-set distance and gives the exact equality dH(C(K),C(L))=dH(K,L). This proves the preservation claim in Lemma 2.4.

    Standard reference: R. Schneider, Convex Bodies: The Brunn–Minkowski Theory, 2nd ed., §1.8, on the Hausdorff metric for convex bodies.

    Complete statement

    Lemma 2.4 (Coordinate reversal, orientation, and endpoint assignment). Let C:ℂ→ℂC:\mathbb C\to\mathbb C be complex conjugation. For every λ∈ℂ\ℝ\lambda\in\mathbb C\setminus\mathbb R and every compact convex polygon PP, C(λP)=λ¯C(P),λP⊆P⇔λ¯C(P)⊆C(P).\begin{equation} \label{eq:coordinate-reversal-intertwining} C(\lambda P)=\overline\lambda\,C(P), \qquad \lambda P\subseteq P \Longleftrightarrow \overline\lambda\,C(P)\subseteq C(P). \end{equation}(2.6) Conjugation preserves the number of extreme points, strictness, relative interiors, supporting incidences, side-intersection properties, image-vertex-on-boundary properties, and Hausdorff-continuous deformation families.

    If xix_i is a positively indexed boundary list for PP and x∼i=x¯−i,\widetilde x_i=\overline{x}_{-i}, then x∼i\widetilde x_i is a positively indexed boundary list for C(P)C(P). However, conjugation reverses the half-open convention: the image of the right-half-open side (xj−1,xj](x_{j-1},x_j] is the left-half-open side [x∼−j,x∼1−j).\begin{equation} \label{eq:coordinate-reversal-half-open} [\widetilde x_{-j},\widetilde x_{1-j}). \end{equation}(2.7) Consequently a contact assignment to right-half-open sides is not, in general, carried to another such assignment by conjugation when endpoint contacts are present. Thus the endpoint convention must be checked again after conjugation.

    ProofOpen the complete proof of Lemma 2.4

    Proof. The identity C(λz)=λ¯C(z)C(\lambda z)=\overline\lambda\,C(z) gives (2.6). Since CC is an invertible real-affine map, it preserves convex combinations, extreme points, faces, relative interiors, and inclusions. A line supports PP if and only if its conjugate supports C(P)C(P), with the conjugate contact face; this proves all incidence, side-intersection, and image-vertex-on-boundary assertions. Applying CC pointwise to a deformation family preserves Hausdorff convergence because CC is an isometry in the adapted metric.

    Complex conjugation reverses the cyclic order on the boundary. Hence x∼i=x¯−i\widetilde x_i=\overline{x}_{-i} restores positive order. The side of C(P)C(P) from x∼−j=x¯j\widetilde x_{-j}=\overline{x}_j to x∼1−j=x¯j−1\widetilde x_{1-j}=\overline{x}_{j-1} is the conjugate of the unoriented segment [xj−1,xj][x_{j-1},x_j]. The old half-open set excludes x¯j−1=x∼1−j\overline{x}_{j-1}=\widetilde x_{1-j} and includes x¯j=x∼−j\overline{x}_j=\widetilde x_{-j}, which is exactly (2.7). Thus conjugation swaps which endpoint is included, proving the final assertion. ◻

    Later arguments must reconstruct the half-open-side assignment after conjugation rather than assume that it is unchanged.

  5. 2.5
    Lemma 2.5

    Origin is interior for a nonreal contraction

    Ensures that the invariant polygon surrounds the origin, making angular order and polarity available.

    First-use vocabularyDefinitions first used in this result
    Supporting functional at a boundary point
    A nonzero real-linear functional ℓ whose maximum or minimum on P is attained at the chosen boundary point. If 0 lies on ∂P, the sign can be chosen so that ℓ≥0 throughout P.
    Finite and infinite rotation order
    The rotation e^{iθ} has finite order when some positive power equals 1; otherwise it has infinite order. A finite orbit sums to zero, while an infinite orbit is dense on the unit circle.
    The orbit used in Lemma 2.5What does “dense in the unit circle” mean, and why is it true?

    A subset of the unit circle is dense when every nonempty open arc contains one of its points. For the rotation Rθ(z)=eiθz, the orbit of a nonzero point is {eikθz:k≥0}. It is finite exactly when θ/(2π) is rational.

    Here is a short reason for the infinite-order case. Consider all integer powers and take their closure H. It is a closed subgroup: it contains 1, is closed under multiplication and inverses, and contains the limits of its convergent sequences. Since the powers are infinite and the circle is compact, quotients of two sufficiently close powers give nonidentity elements of H with arbitrarily small positive angle.

    Fix any angular tolerance ε and choose such an element with angle 0<δ<ε. Its successive powers have angles 0,δ,2δ,… and come within δ of every angle before completing the circle. Thus H comes within ε of every point for every ε>0. Since H is closed, H is the entire circle. Finally, the closure of the nonnegative powers is the same: compactness gives positive powers approaching 1, and multiplying those powers by any fixed negative power approximates that negative power using nonnegative exponents. Hence the forward orbit used in the lemma is dense.

    In Lemma 2.5, the set where the supporting functional ℓ is negative is an open semicircle. Density guarantees that some rotated iterate enters it, contradicting the inequality ℓ(eikθz)≥0.

    Complete statement

    Lemma 2.5 (Interior origin for a nonreal contraction). Let PP be a non-singleton compact convex hull of finitely many points, possibly a segment, and let λ=ρeiθ\lambda=\rho e^{i\theta} with 0<ρ<10<\rho<1 and θ≢0,π(mod⁡2π)\theta\not\equiv0,\pi\pmod{2\pi}. If λP⊆P\lambda P\subseteq P, then PP has nonempty interior and 0∈int⁡(P)0\in\operatorname{int}(P).

    ProofOpen the complete proof of Lemma 2.5

    Proof. For any z∈Pz\in P, invariance gives λkz∈P\lambda^kz\in P, while λkz→0\lambda^kz\to0; since PP is closed, 0∈P0\in P. If PP were a segment, its affine line would pass through 00 and would have to contain the nondegenerate segment λP\lambda P; hence multiplication by eiθe^{i\theta} would preserve a real line, forcing θ≡0\theta\equiv0 or π\pi, a contradiction. Thus PP has nonempty interior.

    Suppose 0∈∂P0\in\partial P. Choose a nonzero real linear functional ℓ\ell such that ℓ≥0\ell\geq0 on PP and choose z∈Pz\in P with ℓ(z)>0\ell(z)>0. Because ρk>0\rho^k>0, it follows that ℓ(eikθz)≥0\ell(e^{ik\theta}z)\geq0 for every k≥0k\geq0. If eiθe^{i\theta} has finite order mm, then m≥3m\geq3 and ∑k=0m−1eikθz=0\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}e^{ik\theta}z=0; summing the displayed nonnegative quantities forces them all to vanish, contrary to ℓ(z)>0\ell(z)>0.     Optional explanationOpen intuition and proof walkthrough

    IntuitionA rotating contraction cannot preserve a nondegenerate polygon confined to one side of the origin: some rotated iterate must eventually enter the opposite open half-plane.

    1. 1

      First put 0 in P

      For z∈P, invariance gives λ^kz∈P. Because |λ|<1, these iterates converge to 0; compactness of P gives 0∈P.

    2. 2

      Exclude a segment

      If P were one-dimensional, its line through 0 would have to be preserved by the nonreal rotation e^{iθ}. A real line is preserved only for angles 0 or π, contrary to ellipticity.

    3. 3

      Assume 0 is on the boundary

      A supporting functional ℓ can be chosen with ℓ≥0 on P and ℓ(z)>0 for some z∈P. Invariance gives λ^kz=ρ^ke^{ikθ}z∈P, hence ρ^kℓ(e^{ikθ}z)≥0. Because ρ^k>0, this forces ℓ(e^{ikθ}z)≥0 for every k.

    4. 4

      Use the rotation orbit

      For finite order, the orbit sums to zero, so nonnegative functional values cannot include a positive one. For infinite order, the orbit is dense on the circle and eventually enters the half-plane ℓ<0. Both cases contradict the support inequality.

    Keep in mindThe origin is not merely fixed by the contraction: it lies strictly inside every nontrivial invariant polygon.

  6. 2.6
    Lemma 2.6

    Oriented order on a convex boundary

    Relates the cyclic order of vertices and sides to the orientation seen from an interior point.

    First-use vocabularyDefinitions first used in this result
    Degree +1
    Every ray from the interior point o meets ∂K exactly once. Traversing ∂K once in its positive orientation therefore makes the direction z−o traverse the unit circle once counterclockwise. Thus the radial map has degree +1 and preserves cyclic order.
    The topological step in Lemma 2.6What does degree +1 mean here?

    A continuous map from one circle to another has an integer degree, its signed number of turns. If a circular parameter t is lifted from angles modulo 2π to real angles, the target angle can be lifted to a continuous function F satisfying

    F(t+2π) =F(t)+2πd.

    The integer d is the degree. Degree +1 means that one positive circuit of the source produces exactly one counterclockwise circuit of the target; degree −1 would reverse the orientation.

    For the radial map πo(z)=(z−o)/|z−o|, every ray from the interior point o meets the boundary of a compact convex set exactly once. The map is therefore a homeomorphism. Traversing the positively oriented boundary keeps the interior on the left and turns the ray once counterclockwise, so its degree is +1 and it preserves cyclic order.

    Complete statement

    Lemma 2.6 (Oriented order on a convex boundary). Let K⊂ℝ2K\subset\mathbb R^2 be a compact convex set with nonempty interior, with ∂K\partial K given its positive orientation. For three distinct noncollinear points a,b,c∈∂Ka,b,c\in\partial K, det⁡(b−a,c−a)>0\begin{equation} \label{eq:oriented-boundary-determinant} \det(b-a,c-a)>0 \end{equation}(2.8) if and only if a,b,ca,b,c occur in that positive cyclic order on ∂K\partial K. Consequently, if QQ is a strict polygon all of whose vertices lie on ∂K\partial K, then the positive cyclic order of the vertices of QQ is exactly the order induced from ∂K\partial K.

    ProofOpen the complete proof of Lemma 2.6

    Proof. Choose o∈int⁡conv⁡({a,b,c})o\in\operatorname{int}\mathop{\mathrm{conv}}(\{a,b,c\}). Since the triangle conv⁡({a,b,c})\mathop{\mathrm{conv}}(\{a,b,c\}) is contained in KK, one has o∈int⁡(K)o\in\operatorname{int}(K). Every ray from oo meets ∂K\partial K in exactly one point, so the radial map πo:∂K→𝕊1,πo(z)=z−o|z−o|,\pi_o:\partial K\longrightarrow\mathbb S^1, \qquad \pi_o(z)=\frac{z-o}{|z-o|}, is a homeomorphism. With the positive boundary orientation it has degree +1+1: every ray from oo meets the boundary exactly once, and traversing the positively oriented boundary once makes the direction z−oz-o traverse the unit circle once counterclockwise. Hence πo\pi_o preserves cyclic order. Because oo lies in the interior of the triangle abcabc, the three rays from oo to a,b,ca,b,c occur counterclockwise in the order a,b,ca,b,c exactly when the oriented area of that triangle is positive, which is (2.8).

    No three vertices of QQ are collinear: a middle point of three collinear vertices would be a convex combination of the other two and therefore would not be extreme. For the final assertion, apply the first part both to KK and to QQ. For every triple of vertices of QQ, the sign of the same determinant characterizes its positive cyclic order on ∂K\partial K and on ∂Q\partial Q. Thus the two ternary cyclic-order relations coincide, and therefore so do the full cyclic orders. ◻

    Strict separation
