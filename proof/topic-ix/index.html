Topic IX · Manuscript pages 76–81

Candidate curves from the Ito equation on Farey intervals

How does the Ito equation determine a unique modulus at each prescribed argument between consecutive Farey fractions?

Topic orientationWhat this chapter proves and why it comes here

Topic VIII defined the Karpelevič region Θₙ from row-stochastic matrices. Topic IX fixes two consecutive Farey fractions and, at each prescribed unit-circle argument between them, uses a strictly increasing real equation to determine one modulus and connect the resulting point to the reduced Ito polynomial.

Endpoint limits and the exceptional order-three real segment are proved before these points are packaged as a closed curve on the Farey interval. Stochastic realization and identification with the boundary of Θₙ are deliberately deferred.

Reading convention. Essential definitions appear before the first statement that needs them. Complete manuscript proofs are closed by default. When a result has an added line-by-line explanation, it appears inside the same disclosure as the proof.

Definitions and arithmetic coordinates

From the Karpelevič region to a Farey interval

A matrix P=(pᵢⱼ) is row-stochastic when pᵢⱼ≥0 and Σⱼpᵢⱼ=1 for every row i. As in Topic VIII, Θₙ is the set of all complex eigenvalues of n×nrow-stochastic matrices.

For t∈[0,1/2], the point exp(2πit) runs once along the upper unit semicircle. At a prescribed parameter x, the ray at argument 2πx is {ρ exp(2πix):ρ≥0}. Fix n≥3. The Farey sequence Fₙ contains every reduced fraction in [0,1] whose denominator is at most n, in increasing order. We use its upper half Fₙ⁺=Fₙ∩[0,1/2]. Two consecutive entries f<g form a Farey interval [f,g]. Fractions are always reduced and denominators are positive. For the formulas, label the endpoint with smaller denominator first:

p/q, r/s, q<s. (II.2.2)

These labels need not follow the left-to-right order f<g.

Choose x∈(f,g) and set

d=⌊n/q⌋, e=s−dq. (II.2.3)

The signed integere is retained; it may be positive, zero, or negative. The positive angular distances used by the scalar equation are

A=2π|qx−p|, B=(2π/d)|sx−r|. (II.2.6)

These angles have the required signs for an elementary reason. Set u=|x−p/q|/|r/s−p/q|∈(0,1). Consecutiveness gives |rq−ps|=1, hence |qx−p|=u/s and |sx−r|=(1−u)/q. Therefore (A+B)/(2π)=u/s+(1−u)/(dq)<1/2because s≥3 anddq≥2.

0<A,B,A+B<π. (II.2.7)

Consequently every sine factor used below is positive.

IX.A

Farey adjacency and the Ito equation

Determinant-one arithmetic identifies adjacent fractions. The Ito polynomial then names the algebraic family. The next result identifies the unique modulus at each prescribed argument.

  1. II.2.1
    Lemma II.2.1Classical result

    Farey adjacency criterion

    Recognizes consecutive fractions using only a determinant and a denominator bound.

    Definitions and notation used here4 entries to consult as needed
    Reduced fraction
    A fraction a/b with b>0 whose numerator and denominator have greatest common divisor one.
    Open an example
    The rational number 2/6 appears in a Farey sequence as the reduced fraction 1/3.
    Farey sequence Fₙ
    All reduced fractions in [0,1] with denominator at most n, written in increasing order.
    Primitive lattice vector
    An integer vector whose coordinates have greatest common divisor one. The reduced fraction a/b corresponds to the primitive vector (b,a).
    Mediant

The radial equation

A unique modulus at the prescribed argument

For the fixed Farey interval and prescribed argument, define Fₓ(ρ)=ρˢ⁄ᵈ sin A+ρᑫ sin B−sin(A+B)on [0,1]. Both exponents and both coefficients of the powers of ρ are positive, so Fₓ is strictly increasing. Proposition II.2.3 proves Fₓ(0)<0<Fₓ(1); consequently it has exactly one zero ρ(x)∈(0,1).

The associated point is λ=ρ exp(2πix). The proof constructs complementary coefficients α and β, fixes one fractional power by an explicit exponential, and derives the reciprocal-coordinate identity. No phrase such as “take the principal root” is left implicit.

    Proposition II.2.4

    Endpoint limits, including the order-three exception
